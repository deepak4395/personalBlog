## Explanation

### Context
This verse marks a significant shift in Krishna's persuasion strategy. Having first appealed to Arjuna's intellect with profound philosophical truths about the immortal soul and the impermanence of the body (Sankhya philosophy), Krishna now turns to more worldly, yet equally powerful, motivators for a warrior prince like Arjuna: honor, duty, and reputation. Arjuna is still overwhelmed by grief and delusion, contemplating abandoning his *dharma* (righteous duty) as a *kshatriya* (warrior) and retreating from the battle. Krishna knows that for a person of Arjuna's stature and upbringing, the fear of dishonor can be a more immediate and compelling force than abstract spiritual knowledge. This verse directly addresses the social and psychological consequences of such a retreat, emphasizing that the shame of cowardice would be a fate worse than death itself for a respected individual.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **अकीर्तिम् (akīrtim)**: Dishonor, infamy, ill-repute. The prefix 'a-' negates 'kīrti' (fame, glory), hence 'not fame.'
*   **च (ca)**: And, also.
*   **अपि (api)**: Even.
*   **भूतानि (bhūtāni)**: Beings, people. Here, it refers to the general populace or future generations.
*   **कथयिष्यन्ति (kathayiṣyanti)**: Will narrate, will speak, will recount (future tense).
*   **ते (te)**: Your (referring to Arjuna).
*   **अव्ययाम् (avyayām)**: Imperishable, eternal, unending. From 'a-' (not) and 'vyaya' (perishable, diminishing). It implies lasting forever.
*   **सम्भावितस्य (sambhāvitasya)**: Of a respected person, of one who is highly esteemed or honored. From 'sambhāva' (respect, honor).
*   **च (ca)**: And.
*   **अकीर्तिः (akīrtiḥ)**: Dishonor, infamy (nominative case).
*   **मरणात् (maraṇāt)**: Than death (ablative case of 'maraṇa').
*   **अतिरिच्यते (atiricyate)**: Exceeds, is worse than, is superior to (in a negative sense here, meaning 'outweighs' or 'is more severe').

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna's words here are a powerful psychological appeal. He highlights that for a person of high esteem, like Arjuna, who has earned respect through his valor and righteousness, a stain on his reputation is more devastating than physical death. This isn't just about what others *think* of him, but how *he* would perceive himself, living with the knowledge of his own perceived cowardice. The concept of `avyayām akīrtim` (eternal infamy) is particularly potent. Physical death is an end; the suffering it entails is finite. But dishonor, especially for a public figure, can persist across generations, affecting one's lineage and legacy. It's a 'social death' or a 'spiritual death' that outlasts the body. For a *kshatriya*, whose *dharma* is inextricably linked with courage, honor, and upholding justice, abandoning the battlefield would be the ultimate dereliction of duty. Krishna is reminding Arjuna that true living for a warrior is not merely existing, but living with honor and fulfilling one's *dharma*. To choose a life of shame over a death of glory is a choice no true warrior could make. This verse underscores the immense value placed on integrity and reputation in ancient Indian society, especially for those in leadership roles, and how it connects to one's overall *dharma*.

### Practical Application
1.  **Integrity in Modern Life:** In today's world, where information spreads rapidly, the importance of maintaining one's integrity and reputation is paramount. Whether in professional dealings, personal relationships, or public discourse, actions that compromise one's principles can lead to lasting dishonor, affecting trust and credibility far more deeply than any momentary gain.
2.  **Choosing Principles Over Comfort:** We often face situations where upholding our values or performing a difficult but necessary duty might be uncomfortable, challenging, or even risky. This verse encourages us to choose the path of integrity and duty, even when it's hard, rather than opting for an easy escape that could lead to long-term regret and a diminished sense of self-worth. For example, a leader might face pressure to make an unethical decision for short-term profit; this verse would prompt them to consider the long-term damage to their reputation and values.
3.  **The Legacy We Build:** Reflect on the legacy you wish to leave behind. Our actions today are the stories of tomorrow. Would you prefer to be remembered for a comfortable life devoid of significant contribution, or for having stood firm in your principles, fulfilling your responsibilities, and facing challenges with courage? This teaching encourages us to live a life that builds a positive, enduring legacy.
