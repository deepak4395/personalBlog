## Explanation

### Context
Verse 2.66 arrives at a crucial juncture in Krishna's discourse on the characteristics of a *sthitaprajna*, a person of steady wisdom. Having previously detailed how sensory attachments lead to delusion and destruction (verses 2.62-63), and how a self-controlled individual finds profound peace (verses 2.64-65), Krishna now elaborates on the dire consequences of *not* being united with the Divine or the true Self. This verse serves as a powerful warning, emphasizing that true understanding, peace, and happiness are not external acquisitions but internal states, fundamentally dependent on an aligned and disciplined mind. It underscores the foundational role of inner connection in spiritual life and daily existence.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **न अस्ति (nāsti)**: There is not, does not exist.
*   **बुद्धिः (buddhiḥ)**: Intellect, discernment, right understanding, discriminating wisdom.
*   **अयुक्तस्य (ayuktasya)**: For the ununited, for one whose mind is not disciplined, integrated, or connected (to the Divine/Self).
*   **न च (na ca)**: Nor, and not.
*   **भावना (bhāvanā)**: Contemplation, spiritual feeling, inner resolve, steady thought, imaginative faculty directed towards higher truths.
*   **अभावयतः (abhāvayataḥ)**: For him who is without contemplation or spiritual feeling.
*   **शान्तिः (śāntiḥ)**: Peace, tranquility, inner calm.
*   **अशान्तस्य (aśāntasya)**: For the unpeaceful, for one who lacks inner peace.
*   **कुतः (kutaḥ)**: Whence, from where, how is it possible?
*   **सुखम् (sukham)**: Happiness, joy, ease, pleasure.

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna meticulously outlines a cascading chain of negative consequences for an individual whose mind is *ayukta* – undisciplined, unconnected, or misaligned with their higher Self or the Divine. This verse is profound because it links seemingly disparate concepts like intellect, contemplation, peace, and happiness into an inseparable spiritual sequence.

First, Krishna states that for an *ayukta* person, there is no *buddhi* (right understanding). This is not to say that an undisciplined person lacks intellectual capacity altogether; rather, they lack *true* or *discriminating* intellect. Their intellectual faculties, however sharp, are clouded by desires, attachments, and ego, leading to flawed judgment and an inability to perceive reality accurately. Without this spiritual discernment, one cannot make choices that lead to lasting well-being.

Following this, the verse declares that for the *ayukta*, there is also no *bhāvanā*. *Bhāvanā* is more than mere thought; it implies a deep inner feeling, a spiritual inclination, or the capacity for sustained contemplative immersion in higher truths. It's the inner resolve that fuels spiritual growth. Without a disciplined mind, this deeper spiritual sensibility cannot develop, leaving the individual adrift in superficiality and sensory distractions.

The absence of *bhāvanā* then leads to the absence of *śānti* (peace). When the mind is not united, when it lacks right understanding and the capacity for spiritual contemplation, it remains restless, agitated by desires, fears, and external circumstances. This inner turmoil prevents any genuine sense of calm.

The final link in the chain is the most striking: for the unpeaceful, how can there be *sukham* (happiness)? Krishna emphatically states that true happiness is utterly unattainable without inner peace. This challenges the common misconception that happiness comes from accumulating possessions, achieving external successes, or indulging in sensory pleasures. The Gita asserts that such external pursuits, if not rooted in inner peace, are fleeting and ultimately unsatisfying. True happiness is an intrinsic state, a byproduct of inner tranquility and spiritual alignment, not an external acquisition.

This verse is central to the Gita's teachings on *yoga* (union), emphasizing that yoga is not just physical postures but an internal state of integration and discipline. It relates to *dharma* because right conduct flows from right understanding, which in turn depends on a disciplined mind. It underscores that the ultimate goal of *moksha* (liberation) is intrinsically linked to profound inner peace and an unwavering connection to the Self.

### Practical Application
1.  **Cultivating Inner Discipline (Yoga in Daily Life)**: Recognize that moments of distraction, restlessness, or impulsive action are signs of an 'ayukta' mind. Consciously work to bring focus and intention to your daily tasks, whether it's work, relationships, or personal growth. Practice mindfulness in simple activities like eating, walking, or listening. This builds the 'yukta' state, leading to clearer *buddhi*.
2.  **Nurturing Contemplation (Bhāvanā)**: Dedicate a few minutes each day to quiet reflection or meditation, even if it's just observing your breath or contemplating a positive quality. This isn't about solving problems but about cultivating an inner space for spiritual feeling and deeper insight. This practice strengthens *bhāvanā* and helps quiet the mind's incessant chatter.
3.  **Prioritizing Inner Peace over External Gains**: Before reacting to a challenging situation or chasing a new desire, ask yourself: "Will this action or pursuit genuinely contribute to my inner peace?" Learn to discern between temporary gratification and lasting contentment. Recognizing that true happiness stems from inner peace can shift your priorities from external acquisition to internal cultivation.

### Reflection
Am I constantly seeking happiness externally, or am I cultivating the inner conditions for it? How does my level of inner discipline (being 'yukta' or 'ayukta') impact my clarity of thought and my overall sense of well-being? Where do I find my peace, and is it a lasting peace?

## Key Concepts
*   **The Interconnectedness of Inner States**: Intellect, contemplation, peace, and happiness are not isolated but form a vital chain, each dependent on the preceding one.
*   **The Primacy of Inner Discipline (Yoga)**: Being 'yukta' (united, disciplined) is the foundational requirement for true wisdom and spiritual insight.
*   **Peace as a Prerequisite for Happiness**: Lasting happiness (*sukham*) is an outcome of inner peace (*śānti*), which cannot be attained by an undisciplined mind.
*   **True Intellect (Buddhi) vs. Mere Intelligence**: *Buddhi* here implies discerning wisdom that arises from spiritual alignment, not just cognitive ability.
