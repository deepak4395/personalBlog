## Explanation

### Context
Chapter 4 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled "Jnana Karma Sanyasa Yoga" (The Yoga of Renunciation of Action in Knowledge), is a pivotal chapter where Lord Krishna begins to unveil deeper truths about His divine nature and the origin of spiritual knowledge. Having established in the preceding verses (4.1-3) that this eternal yoga was first taught by Him to Vivasvan, then to Manu, and then to Ikshvaku, Krishna emphasizes the ancient lineage and the eventual loss of this knowledge over time. Arjuna, puzzled by Krishna's claim of teaching ancient sages while being a contemporary, prompts Krishna to reveal His divine, unborn nature in verses 4.4-6. It is against this backdrop of Krishna's self-revelation as the Supreme Being, unborn and imperishable, yet appearing in the material world, that verse 4.7 provides the profound reason and purpose for such divine descents or incarnations (avataras). This verse clarifies that divine intervention is not arbitrary but a direct response to a fundamental imbalance in the cosmic order.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
Let's break down the profound statements within this shloka:

*   **`yadā yadā` (Whenever, whenever):** This repetition emphasizes the recurring nature of the phenomenon, signifying that this is not a one-time event but a principle of cosmic governance.
*   **`hi` (indeed, certainly):** Adds emphasis and certainty to Krishna's declaration.
*   **`dharmasya` (of righteousness, of cosmic law):** Dharma is a multifaceted concept encompassing cosmic order, moral law, duty, virtue, truth, and the inherent nature of existence. It represents the principles that sustain the universe and human society.
*   **`glāniḥ` (decline, decay, degeneration, weakening):** Refers to the deterioration or weakening of these principles.
*   **`bhavati` (happens, occurs):** Indicates the coming into being of this decline.
*   **`bhārata` (O descendant of Bharata, Arjuna):** A respectful address to Arjuna, highlighting his lineage and underscoring the importance of the teaching.
*   **`abhyutthānam` (uprising, predominance, ascendancy, increase):** Signifies the rise or strengthening of something.
*   **`adharmasya` (of unrighteousness, of chaos):** Adharma is the antithesis of Dharma—it represents disorder, immorality, injustice, and actions contrary to cosmic law.
*   **`tadā` (then, at that time):** Indicates the consequence or response to the preceding conditions.
*   **`ātmānaṁ` (My own self, My being, My form):** Refers to Krishna's divine essence or form.
*   **`sṛjāmi` (I create, I manifest, I embody):** Implies a conscious, deliberate act of bringing Himself into manifest existence. It's not a birth in the ordinary sense but a self-manifestation.
*   **`aham` (I):** Reinforces the personal, direct action of the Supreme Lord.

### Deeper Understanding
This verse is one of the most celebrated and fundamental declarations in the Bhagavad Gita, encapsulating the very purpose of divine incarnation (Avatara). Krishna unequivocally states that His manifestation in the material world is not a random occurrence but a deliberate, cyclical response to the cosmic imbalance created by the decline of Dharma and the rise of Adharma.

The core teaching here is about divine providence and the active role of the Supreme Being in maintaining cosmic order. Krishna is not a distant, uninvolved deity; rather, He actively intervenes when the fabric of righteousness is threatened. This intervention is not out of necessity, as Krishna is beyond all limitations, but out of His boundless compassion for living beings and His commitment to uphold the principles that sustain creation.

The interplay of Dharma and Adharma is central. Dharma represents the natural order, the principles of truth, justice, morality, and spiritual conduct that lead to harmony and well-being. Adharma, conversely, signifies chaos, untruth, injustice, and immorality, leading to suffering and degradation. When Adharma gains ascendancy, it creates an environment where virtuous living becomes difficult, and spiritual progress is hampered. It is at such critical junctures that the divine manifests.

This verse lays the philosophical groundwork for understanding the concept of Avatara in Hinduism. An Avatara is not merely a human being but a direct descent of the divine consciousness into the material realm, retaining full divine awareness and power, for a specific purpose. The subsequent verse (4.8) will elaborate on this purpose: to protect the righteous, annihilate the wicked, and re-establish Dharma. This provides immense comfort and reassurance to spiritual seekers and humanity at large, knowing that ultimate justice and order will always be restored by the Supreme Will.

### Practical Application
This profound teaching, while describing a cosmic phenomenon, holds significant practical implications for modern readers:

1.  **Recognizing the Decline of Dharma:** In our contemporary world, we can observe various forms of "glāniḥ dharmasya" – a decline in ethical standards, truthfulness, compassion, and justice. Corruption, environmental degradation, social injustice, and widespread untruth are all manifestations of Adharma. This verse encourages us to be discerning observers of our society and our own lives, recognizing where righteousness is faltering.
2.  **Upholding Personal Dharma:** While we may not be able to single-handedly solve global issues, the principle of upholding Dharma begins at the individual level. Each person has a *svadharma* (personal duty/righteousness) based on their nature and circumstances. By living truthfully, acting ethically, fulfilling responsibilities, and practicing compassion, we contribute to the collective Dharma, however small. Even amidst widespread Adharma, one's steadfastness in Dharma acts as a beacon.
3.  **Source of Hope and Resilience:** The verse offers a powerful message of hope. It assures us that darkness and chaos are never ultimate. Whenever Adharma reaches its peak, divine intervention, directly or indirectly through awakened individuals, will inevitably occur to restore balance. This fosters resilience and prevents despair, reminding us that there is a higher order and purpose at play, even when things seem dire. It inspires us to be instruments of that divine will, striving for positive change.

## Key Concepts

*   **Dharma and Adharma:** The fundamental cosmic principles of righteousness/order and unrighteousness/chaos, respectively, whose balance dictates the state of existence.
*   **Divine Incarnation (Avatara):** The deliberate descent or manifestation of the Supreme Being into the material world, not through ordinary birth, but through His own divine power.
*   **Cosmic Balance:** The idea that the universe operates on a principle of equilibrium, and divine intervention occurs to restore this balance when it is severely disturbed.
*   **Divine Providence:** The concept that a higher power actively guides and maintains the order of the universe, offering reassurance and hope in times of crisis.
