## Explanation

### Context
This verse is a crucial part of Krishna's discourse on *Sankhya Yoga* in Chapter 2, specifically detailing the characteristics of a *sthitaprajña* (person of steady wisdom). After defining who a *sthitaprajña* is and how they behave, Krishna provides vivid analogies to illustrate the inner state of such an enlightened individual. Verse 69 contrasted the "night" and "day" of the enlightened and unenlightened. Verse 70 continues this theme by explaining how a *sthitaprajña* deals with desires, using the metaphor of the ocean, which is a powerful image of vastness, depth, and unwavering stability. This sets the stage for the final verses of the chapter, which culminate in the description of *brahmi sthiti* (the state of Brahman).

*   **अचलप्रतिष्ठम् (acala-pratiṣṭham):** Immovable, steady, firmly established. Despite being filled, the ocean does not overflow or lose its composure.

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna uses a profound analogy to differentiate between a truly peaceful individual and one who is perpetually restless. The ocean, despite being constantly fed by rivers, maintains its vastness and does not overflow or become agitated. Its depth and expanse allow it to absorb all incoming waters without losing its inherent nature.

Similarly, a wise person (the *sthitaprajña*) is like this ocean. Desires (*kāmāḥ*) will inevitably arise in the mind; it is the nature of the mind to generate thoughts and desires. The key is not to suppress desires forcefully, which is often futile, but to cultivate an inner state where these desires, when they arise, do not disturb the inner tranquility. The *sthitaprajña* observes desires without getting entangled, without identifying with them, and without being compelled to act upon them. They do not chase after desires (*na kāmakāmī*); rather, desires enter their consciousness and are absorbed, much like rivers merge into the ocean, without causing agitation.

This teaching relates deeply to the concept of *niskama karma* (action without attachment to results). A person who is *kāmakāmī* is constantly driven by the pursuit of gratification, leading to a cycle of expectation, fleeting pleasure, disappointment, and renewed craving. This pursuit is inherently restless and never leads to lasting peace. True peace (*śānti*) comes from an inner state of contentment and non-attachment, where the external world and its temptations do not dictate one's inner equilibrium. It's about shifting focus from external fulfillment to internal self-sufficiency. This verse underscores that peace is not the absence of external stimuli or desires, but the ability to remain undisturbed by them.

### Practical Application
1.  **Observe Desires, Don't Be Them:** When a desire arises (e.g., for a new gadget, a certain food, or recognition), instead of immediately acting on it or feeling deprived, practice observing it. Acknowledge its presence without identification. Ask yourself, "Is this desire truly essential for my well-being or just a passing impulse?" This creates a gap between the desire and your reaction, allowing for conscious choice.
2.  **Cultivate Inner Contentment:** Focus on what you have and appreciate it, rather than constantly striving for what you lack. Practice gratitude daily. This builds an inner reservoir of peace, making you less susceptible to the turbulence of external desires. Just as the ocean is full, cultivate a sense of inner fullness.
3.  **Mindful Engagement, Not Avoidance:** This verse does not advocate for renouncing all action or desires in a reclusive manner. Rather, it suggests engaging with life and its experiences mindfully. One can fulfill responsibilities, pursue goals, and enjoy life's pleasures, but with a detached awareness, ensuring that the pursuit itself does not become a source of agitation or attachment. The goal is to act from a place of inner stability, not from a reactive craving.

## Key Concepts

*   **Inner Equanimity:** The state of remaining calm and undisturbed despite external or internal stimuli, particularly desires.
*   **Non-attachment to Desires:** Understanding that desires will arise, but not identifying with them or being driven by them for happiness.
*   **True Peace (Śānti):** A profound, lasting state of tranquility that comes from within, not from the fulfillment of external desires.
*   **The Sthitaprajña Ideal:** This verse further elaborates on the characteristics of a person of steady wisdom, who embodies inner stability and freedom from desire-driven agitation.
