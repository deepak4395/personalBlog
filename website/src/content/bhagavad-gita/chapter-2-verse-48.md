## Explanation

### Context
This profound verse, 2.48, stands as a cornerstone in Krishna's teachings on Karma Yoga within the Bhagavad Gita's second chapter, 'Sankhya Yoga.' Following verse 2.47, which famously states, "You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of action," Krishna now elaborates on *how* one should perform these duties. He moves from stating *what* not to do (be attached to results) to *how* to be while acting: established in Yoga and maintaining equanimity. It’s a practical instruction for Arjuna, who is grappling with the moral dilemma of fighting his kin, guiding him towards action free from the binding effects of karma.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **योगस्थः (yoga-sthaḥ):** Established in Yoga; steadfast in the state of Yoga. Here, Yoga implies a state of inner balance, spiritual connection, and skillful action.
*   **कुरु (kuru):** Perform; do.
*   **कर्माणि (karmāṇi):** Actions; duties; prescribed activities.
*   **सङ्गं (saṅgaṁ):** Attachment; clinging; desire for the fruits of action.
*   **त्यक्त्वा (tyaktvā):** Having abandoned; having given up.
*   **धनञ्जय (dhanañjaya):** O Arjuna (a name meaning 'conqueror of wealth,' addressing Arjuna's prowess and noble nature).
*   **सिद्ध्यसिद्धयोः (siddhy-asiddhyoḥ):** In success and failure; in attainment and non-attainment; in accomplishment and non-accomplishment.
*   **समः (samaḥ):** Equal; same; equipoised; balanced.
*   **भूत्वा (bhūtvā):** Having become.
*   **समत्वं (samatvaṁ):** Equanimity; evenness of mind; balance.
*   **योग (yoga):** Yoga; spiritual discipline; union; skill in action.
*   **उच्यते (ucyate):** Is called; is said to be.

### Deeper Understanding
This verse offers a revolutionary definition of Yoga, moving beyond mere physical postures or meditation techniques to encompass a mental state applicable to all aspects of life. Krishna teaches Arjuna, and by extension all of humanity, how to engage fully with the world without becoming entangled by its dualities.

*   **What is Krishna teaching Arjuna?** Krishna is instructing Arjuna to perform his duties (dharma) as a warrior, not with an eye on victory or defeat, but from a state of inner equipoise. The emphasis is on the *process* and the *attitude* during action, rather than the outcome. By abandoning attachment (saṅgaṁ tyaktvā) to success (siddhi) or failure (asiddhi), one can act with clarity and freedom. This detachment is not indifference, but a higher form of engagement – one where the mind remains steady, unaffected by external fluctuations.

*   **Key Concepts Presented:**
    *   **Establishment in Yoga (`yoga-sthaḥ`):** This is the prerequisite. It means acting from a place of inner stillness and spiritual awareness, rather than being driven by ego, fear, or desire. It implies a conscious connection to one's higher self or the Divine.
    *   **Abandoning Attachment (`saṅgaṁ tyaktvā`):** This is the core instruction for Karma Yoga. Attachment to results binds the individual to the cycle of karma. When one acts with attachment, the mind is constantly oscillating between hope and fear, joy and sorrow, depending on the outcome. Giving up this attachment frees the mind to focus purely on the action itself.
    *   **Equanimity in Success and Failure (`siddhy-asiddhyoḥ samaḥ bhūtvā`):** This is the practical manifestation of detachment. A person established in Yoga treats both positive and negative outcomes with an equal mind. Success doesn't lead to arrogance, nor does failure lead to despair. Both are seen as transient experiences, part of the natural flow of life, and opportunities for learning.
    *   **Equanimity is Yoga (`samatvaṁ yoga ucyate`):** This is the profound definition. True Yoga is not just about specific practices but about cultivating an evenness of mind, a balanced perspective that remains undisturbed by the ups and downs of life. This mental stability is what truly liberates one from the reactions of karma, making all actions a form of spiritual practice.

*   **Relation to Dharma, Karma, Yoga:** This verse is central to the concept of Karma Yoga. It shows how one can fulfill their dharma (duty) without accumulating binding karma. By acting with `samatvaṁ` (equanimity) and `tyaktvā saṅgaṁ` (abandoning attachment), actions become `akarma` (non-binding actions), leading to spiritual liberation. This skilled approach to action (often linked to verse 2.50, `yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam` – Yoga is skill in action) transforms mundane tasks into opportunities for spiritual growth and self-realization.

### Practical Application
The teaching of equanimity is incredibly relevant for modern life, which is often characterized by extreme highs and lows, constant pressure for success, and fear of failure.

1.  **Professional Life:** In a competitive work environment, it's easy to get caught up in the pursuit of promotions, bonuses, or recognition. Applying this teaching means focusing on delivering your best work, honing your skills, and contributing meaningfully, without becoming overly invested in the specific outcome. If a project succeeds, appreciate it without ego; if it faces setbacks, learn from them without self-blame. This approach reduces stress and fosters sustained productivity.
2.  **Personal Goals and Hobbies:** Whether it's learning a new skill, training for a marathon, or pursuing a creative endeavor, maintain an attitude of equanimity. Dedicate yourself to the practice, enjoy the process, and accept the natural ups and downs. If you achieve your goal, celebrate modestly; if you encounter difficulties or don't meet an expectation, don't let it deter you or define your self-worth. The joy is in the journey, not just the destination.
3.  **Relationships:** Engage in relationships with love, honesty, and effort, but without intense attachment to how the other person 'should' behave or how the relationship 'must' turn out. Provide support, listen actively, and express care, but understand that you cannot control others' actions or the ultimate trajectory of a relationship. This helps in navigating conflicts, managing expectations, and reducing emotional dependency, leading to healthier and more resilient connections.

### Reflection
*   How do I typically react to success and failure in my daily life? Am I truly balanced?
*   What attachments do I hold to the outcomes of my actions? Can I identify them and consciously work to release them?
*   How can I cultivate a mindset of 'yoga-sthaḥ' – being established in an inner state of balance – before, during, and after my actions?

## Key Concepts

*   **Karma Yoga:** The path of selfless action, where duties are performed without attachment to results.
*   **Detachment (Vairagya/Anasakti):** The spiritual practice of releasing clinging to outcomes, desires, and material possessions.
*   **Equanimity (Samatvam):** A state of mental balance and inner peace, remaining undisturbed by dualities like success/failure, pleasure/pain.
*   **Skill in Action (Yogaḥ Karmasu Kauśalam):** Though from a later verse (2.50), this concept is intrinsically linked, implying that equanimity makes one's actions more effective and less binding.
