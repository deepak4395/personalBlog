## Explanation

### Context
Verse 2.65 of the Bhagavad Gita comes as a profound declaration following a crucial discussion on the perils of uncontrolled senses and the path to inner discipline. In the preceding verses (2.62-63), Krishna meticulously outlined the destructive chain reaction that begins with dwelling on sense objects, leading to attachment, desire, anger, delusion, loss of memory, destruction of intelligence, and ultimately, spiritual ruin. Verse 2.64 then presented the solution: a disciplined individual, free from attachment and aversion, who engages with sense objects while keeping the senses under control, attains `prasāda` – a state of inner serenity. This current verse, 2.65, now elaborates on the glorious fruits of attaining this `prasāda`, describing it as the ultimate antidote to all suffering and the foundation for a stable, discerning intellect. It is a pivotal point, transitioning from the 'what not to do' and 'how to do it' to the 'what happens when you do it right'.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **प्रसादे (prasāde):** In serenity, tranquility, clarity, grace, favor. This is a highly significant term. While it can mean divine grace, in this context, it refers to an internal state of mental calm, purity, and peace achieved through self-mastery, free from agitation and disturbance.
*   **सर्वदुःखानां (sarvaduḥkhānāṁ):** Of all sorrows, pains, sufferings, miseries. 'Sarva' means all, and 'duḥkha' refers to suffering or unease.
*   **हानिः (hāniḥ):** Destruction, loss, cessation, disappearance.
*   **अस्य (asya):** Of him/her; belonging to such a person.
*   **उपजायते (upajāyate):** Arises, comes into being, takes place. Here, it implies that the destruction of sorrow naturally arises or occurs.
*   **प्रसन्नचेतसः (prasannacetasaḥ):** Of one whose mind (cetas) is tranquil, serene, clear, cheerful, or প্রসন্ন (prasanna). This describes the state of the individual who experiences `prasāda`.
*   **हि (hi):** Indeed, certainly, verily. Emphasizes the certainty of the statement.
*   **आशु (āśu):** Soon, quickly, immediately.
*   **बुद्धिः (buddhiḥ):** Intellect, discriminative faculty, reason, understanding.
*   **पर्यवतिष्ठते (paryavatiṣṭhate):** Becomes steady, firmly established, well-settled, unwavering, fixed.

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna here teaches that `prasāda` is not merely a pleasant feeling but a transformative state that eradicates the very roots of suffering. When the mind achieves this serenity, all `duḥkha` – whether physical, mental, or spiritual – ceases to exist for that individual. This is not to say that external challenges disappear, but rather that the internal reaction to them changes. A person in `prasāda` is unperturbed by the dualities of life (gain/loss, pleasure/pain, honor/dishonor) because their inner state is independent of external circumstances.

The most profound outcome mentioned is `buddhiḥ paryavatiṣṭhate` – the intellect becomes steady. An unsteady intellect, as described in earlier verses (2.41, 2.44), is characterized by being swayed by desires, doubts, and sensory distractions. It cannot make firm decisions or discern truth from illusion. When the mind is serene (`prasannacetasaḥ`), the intellect becomes sharp, clear, and unshakeable. This steady intellect is crucial for a `sthita-prajñā` (person of steady wisdom), enabling them to perceive reality accurately, understand their `dharma` (righteous duty), and act without attachment to results. This directly links to the core themes of Karma Yoga and Jnana Yoga, where right action and right knowledge are paramount, both requiring a stable and discerning intellect. The cessation of sorrow and the stabilization of the intellect are thus two sides of the same coin, representing the pinnacle of mental discipline and the foundation for spiritual liberation.

### Practical Application
1.  **Mindful Observation of Reactions:** Instead of immediately reacting to frustrating or pleasing external stimuli, pause and observe your internal state. Ask yourself if your mind is truly serene. This practice of creating a gap between stimulus and response helps cultivate `prasāda`. For example, if someone criticizes you, instead of immediately feeling defensive or angry, take a deep breath, observe the sensation in your body, and consciously choose a calm, non-reactive response.
2.  **Cultivating Contentment (Santosha):** Actively practice gratitude and focus on what you have rather than constantly chasing what you lack. The endless pursuit of desires is a primary source of mental agitation. By finding contentment in the present, you reduce the 'duḥkha' caused by unfulfilled desires and foster inner peace. A daily gratitude journal or a few minutes of reflecting on positive aspects of your life can significantly contribute to a `prasannacetas`.
3.  **Making Decisions from a Calm State:** Avoid making significant decisions when you are emotionally agitated, angry, or overly excited. A steady intellect (buddhi) is essential for wise choices. If faced with a crucial decision, take time to calm your mind through meditation, a walk in nature, or simply stepping away from the situation until a state of clarity and serenity returns. This ensures your decisions are rooted in reason, not fleeting emotions.

## Key Concepts

*   **Prasāda (Serenity/Tranquility):** An internal state of mental calm, purity, and peace, free from agitation.
*   **Duḥkha-hāni (Cessation of Sorrow):** The complete destruction or disappearance of all forms of suffering.
*   **Prasannacetasaḥ (Tranquil Mind):** The characteristic of an individual whose mind is serene, clear, and unperturbed.
*   **Sthita-prajñā (Steady Intellect):** The ultimate outcome where the discriminative faculty becomes firm, unwavering, and capable of clear perception and wise decision-making.
