## Explanation

### Context
In the previous verse (3.1), Arjuna expressed his confusion. Krishna had previously extolled the virtues of both the path of knowledge (Sankhya Yoga, involving renunciation of action) and the path of action (Karma Yoga, involving selfless action). Arjuna, still grappling with the dilemma of whether to fight or renounce, found these teachings perplexing. If knowledge is superior to action, he wondered, why was Krishna urging him to engage in a dreadful war? This verse (3.2) is Arjuna's direct and earnest plea for clarity. He feels that Krishna's words, while profound, are intermingled or contradictory, causing his intellect to waver. He desires a single, unambiguous instruction that will lead him to the ultimate welfare (śreyas).

This plea is crucial because it sets the stage for Krishna to elaborate extensively on Karma Yoga as the practical and accessible path for most individuals, especially those still engaged in the world. It highlights the common human tendency to seek a singular, clear directive when faced with complex spiritual truths, rather than embracing the nuanced interplay of different paths.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **व्यामिश्रेण (vyāmiśreṇa)**: 'by mixed,' 'by intermingled,' 'by perplexing.' It implies a combination of ideas that seem to contradict each other.
*   **इव (iva)**: 'as if,' 'as it were,' 'apparently.' Arjuna uses this to soften his statement, not directly accusing Krishna but expressing his subjective experience of confusion.
*   **वाक्येन (vākyena)**: 'by words,' 'by statements.' Refers to Krishna's previous teachings.
*   **बुद्धिम् (buddhim)**: 'intellect,' 'understanding,' 'reason.' Arjuna's faculty of discernment.
*   **मोहयसि (mohayasi)**: 'You bewilder,' 'You delude,' 'You confuse.'
*   **मे (me)**: 'my.'
*   **तत् (tat)**: 'therefore,' 'that.' Refers to the path.
*   **एकम् (ekam)**: 'one,' 'single.' Arjuna desires a singular, unambiguous path.
*   **वद (vada)**: 'tell,' 'speak.' An imperative, a direct request.
*   **निश्चित्य (niścitya)**: 'having ascertained,' 'having decided firmly,' 'decisively.' Arjuna wants a definitive answer.
*   **येन (yena)**: 'by which,' 'whereby.'
*   **श्रेयः (śreyaḥ)**: 'the highest good,' 'spiritual welfare,' 'ultimate benefit.' This is not merely worldly pleasure (preyas) but lasting spiritual well-being.
*   **अहम् (aham)**: 'I.'
*   **आप्नुयाम् (āpnuyām)**: 'may attain,' 'may achieve.'

Together, Arjuna is saying, "Your words seem to confuse my intellect, so please tell me one definite path by which I can attain the highest good."

### Deeper Understanding
Arjuna's confusion is not a sign of weakness but a natural human response to profound spiritual teachings that transcend simplistic dualities. Krishna had previously stated that both Sankhya (knowledge/renunciation) and Yoga (action/Karma Yoga) lead to the same supreme state. However, to Arjuna's still-materially-oriented mind, these appeared as two distinct, even conflicting, paths. He yearns for a clear, singular instruction, a 'straight line' to liberation, because his intellect is not yet ready to reconcile the apparent paradoxes.

Krishna's teaching is never truly confusing; it is comprehensive. He presents different facets of truth to cater to different temperaments and stages of spiritual evolution. Arjuna, as a warrior (Kshatriya), is naturally inclined to action, but his attachment to the fruits of action and his aversion to violence are causing his internal conflict. This verse highlights the universal quest for a definitive path to ultimate well-being (śreyas), distinguishing it from temporary worldly pleasures (preyas). Arjuna's sincerity in seeking this highest good is evident. He doesn't ask for an easier path, but a clear one that guarantees spiritual progress.

This verse also subtly introduces the idea that while the ultimate goal is the same, the *means* to achieve it can vary, and one path might be more suitable for a particular individual based on their nature (svabhāva) and stage of life. Krishna will use this opportunity to firmly establish Karma Yoga as the recommended path for Arjuna and, by extension, for most people in the world who cannot simply renounce action.

### Practical Application
1.  **Seeking Clarity in Overwhelm:** In modern life, we are constantly bombarded with information, advice, and options for self-improvement, career, or spiritual growth. This can lead to a feeling of 'vyāmiśreṇa vākyena' – mixed-up words that bewilder our intellect. Arjuna's example teaches us to pause, acknowledge our confusion, and earnestly seek clarity from reliable sources or trusted mentors, asking for the *one decisive path* relevant to our current situation and highest good.
2.  **Focusing on 'Shreyas' over 'Preyas':** Arjuna asks for the path to 'śreyas' (highest good), not just 'preyas' (immediate gratification). This is a vital distinction in decision-making. Before embarking on any path, we should ask ourselves: Does this lead to lasting well-being, spiritual growth, and inner peace, or merely temporary pleasure and fleeting success? Prioritizing long-term 'śreyas' helps cut through confusion.
3.  **Humble Inquiry:** Arjuna's tone is respectful and humble, despite his confusion. When seeking guidance, approaching teachers or wisdom traditions with humility, acknowledging our limitations, and clearly articulating our dilemma, as Arjuna does, opens the door for effective learning and receives clearer direction.

### Reflection

*   Am I truly prioritizing my 'highest good' (śreyas) in my decisions, or am I often swayed by immediate gratification (preyas)?
*   How can I cultivate a more humble and focused approach when seeking wisdom and guidance?
