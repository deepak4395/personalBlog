## Explanation

### Context
Verse 2.51 arrives at a pivotal moment in Krishna's discourse to Arjuna, immediately following the profound teachings on *Buddhi Yoga*, the Yoga of Intellect or Selfless Action. In the preceding verses (2.47-50), Krishna has introduced the revolutionary concept of performing one's duty without attachment to the results. He famously declared, "To action alone hast thou a right and never at all to its fruits; let not the fruits of action be thy motive; neither let there be in thee any attachment to inaction" (2.47). He then emphasized the importance of equanimity (*samatva*) in success and failure, stating that such equanimity is yoga itself (2.48). Verse 50 further solidified this by stating that those endowed with this spiritual intelligence transcend both good and bad actions. Verse 51 now presents the ultimate, transformative consequence of embracing this path: liberation from the cycle of birth and death and attainment of a state of supreme peace.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **कर्मजं (karmajam):** 'Born of action,' 'resulting from action.' This refers to the outcomes, consequences, or fruits that naturally arise from any performed action.
*   **बुद्धियुक्ताः (buddhiyuktāḥ):** 'Endowed with *Buddhi*,' 'united by intellect.' Here, *Buddhi* is not merely intellectual understanding but a higher, discerning spiritual intelligence that enables one to perform actions with equanimity and detachment from results. It implies a state of mental equipoise and wisdom in action.
*   **हि (hi):** 'Indeed,' 'certainly.' An emphatic particle, underscoring the truth of the statement.
*   **फलं (phalam):** 'Fruit,' 'result,' 'outcome.' The specific returns or rewards expected from one's efforts.
*   **त्यक्त्वा (tyaktvā):** 'Having renounced,' 'having abandoned,' 'having given up.' It signifies a conscious letting go of attachment, not of the action itself, but of the desire for specific outcomes.
*   **मनीषिणः (manīṣiṇaḥ):** 'The wise,' 'the discerning ones,' 'the thoughtful.' Those who possess deep insight and understanding, recognizing the transient nature of worldly results.
*   **जन्मबन्धविनिर्मुक्ताः (janma-bandha-vinirmuktāḥ):** 'Freed from the bondage of birth.' This is a compound word: `janma` (birth), `bandha` (bondage, fetter), `vinirmuktāḥ` (freed, liberated). It directly refers to liberation from *samsara*, the cycle of repeated birth and death, a central concept in Indian philosophy.
*   **पदं (padam):** 'State,' 'abode,' 'goal,' 'destination.'
*   **गच्छन्ति (gacchanti):** 'They attain,' 'they go to.'
*   **अनामयम् (anāmayam):** 'Beyond misery,' 'without illness,' 'free from suffering,' 'a state of perfect peace and well-being.' This describes the ultimate, blissful state of liberation.

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna's teaching in this verse is a profound articulation of the path of Karma Yoga. He is not advocating for inaction or indifference, but for a radical shift in one's relationship with action. The 'spiritual intelligence' (*Buddhi*) mentioned here is the capacity to act with full engagement and skill, yet without the mental agitation caused by craving for results or fear of failure. It is a state of inner freedom and equanimity that allows one to perform their *dharma* (duty) purely for the sake of the action itself, or for a higher purpose, rather than for personal gain.

The core concept is the 'renunciation of the fruits of action' (*karma-phala-tyaga*). This doesn't mean becoming lazy or careless; on the contrary, it implies performing actions with even greater focus and dedication, as the mind is no longer distracted by the anxieties of outcome. The 'wise' (*manīṣiṇaḥ*) are those who understand the impermanence of material rewards and the true nature of the Self, recognizing that true fulfillment lies beyond the fleeting successes and failures of the material world.

The ultimate reward for such a life is `janma-bandha-vinirmuktāḥ` – liberation from the cycle of birth and death. According to the Gita, it is attachment to results and the subsequent accumulation of *karma* that binds the soul to *samsara*. By acting selflessly, one ceases to generate binding *karma*, effectively cutting the ties that lead to rebirth. This liberation culminates in attaining `padam anāmayam`, a state of eternal peace, absolute freedom from suffering, and union with the Divine. This is *moksha*, the highest spiritual goal.

### Practical Application
1.  **In the Professional Sphere:** Imagine a professional working on a project. Instead of constantly worrying about promotions, recognition, or the project's ultimate success (which may be influenced by many external factors), they focus entirely on performing their tasks with excellence, integrity, and dedication. They pour their best effort into the work itself, detached from the ego's demand for specific outcomes. This approach reduces stress, enhances focus, and often leads to better results, paradoxically.
2.  **In Relationships:** When engaging with family or friends, we often have expectations about how they should behave or respond. Applying this teaching means performing acts of love, kindness, or service without attachment to their reaction or whether they reciprocate. The joy comes from the act of giving itself, fostering healthier, less demanding relationships.
3.  **Personal Goals and Creative Pursuits:** Whether it's learning a new skill, pursuing a hobby, or engaging in artistic creation, we often get bogged down by the desire for perfection, external validation, or immediate success. Practicing *karma-phala-tyaga* means enjoying the process of learning or creating, focusing on the effort and the inherent joy of the activity, rather than being solely driven by the end product or its reception. This allows for greater creativity and resilience in the face of setbacks.

## Key Concepts
*   **Karma Yoga:** The path of selfless action, performing one's duties without attachment to the results.
*   **Buddhi Yoga:** The yoga of spiritual intelligence, guiding actions with discernment and equanimity.
*   **Renunciation of Fruits of Action (Karma-phala-tyaga):** The practice of letting go of desires for specific outcomes, while remaining fully engaged in the action.
*   **Liberation (Moksha/Janma-bandha-vinirmuktāḥ):** Freedom from the cycle of birth and death, achieved by transcending the binding effects of *karma*.
