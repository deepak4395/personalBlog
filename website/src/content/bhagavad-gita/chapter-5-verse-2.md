## Explanation

### Context
In Chapter 5, titled 'Karma Sanyasa Yoga' (The Yoga of Renunciation of Action), Arjuna once again expresses his confusion to Krishna. In the very first verse, he asks Krishna to definitively tell him which path is superior: the renunciation of actions (sannyasa) or the performance of actions (karma yoga). This question arises from Krishna's previous teachings which, at times, seemed to advocate for renunciation of attachment to action, and at others, for the performance of one's duty without attachment. Arjuna, ever the seeker of clarity, wants a clear choice between what appears to be two distinct paths. This verse (5.2) is Krishna's direct answer to that fundamental question, setting the stage for the rest of the chapter where he elaborates on the nature of true renunciation and action.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **श्रीभगवानुवाच (śrībhagavānuvāca)**: The Supreme Lord said.
*   **संन्यासः (saṁnyāsaḥ)**: Renunciation; specifically, the renunciation of actions or the results of actions. It implies a detachment from worldly affairs.
*   **कर्मयोगश्च (karmayogaśca)**: And Karma Yoga; the path of selfless action, performing one's duties without attachment to the fruits thereof.
*   **निःश्रेयसकरौ (niḥśreyasakarau)**: Both lead to the supreme good or liberation. `niḥśreyasa` means the highest good, ultimate liberation, or salvation.
*   **परौ (parau)**: Both, supreme (in the sense of being significant or leading to the ultimate goal).
*   **तयोस्तु (tayostu)**: But of these two.
*   **कर्मसंन्यासात् (karmasaṁnyāsāt)**: Than the renunciation of actions. This implies a comparison.
*   **कर्मयोगो (karmayogo)**: Karma Yoga; the path of selfless action.
*   **विशिष्यते (viśiṣyate)**: Is superior, is distinguished, is preferred.

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna's declaration in this verse is pivotal. He acknowledges that both paths – sannyasa (renunciation) and karma yoga (action in devotion) – ultimately lead to the `niḥśreyasa`, the supreme good or liberation. This is a crucial point, indicating that the *goal* is the same, regardless of the external form of spiritual practice. However, he then explicitly states that `karma yoga` is superior to `karmasaṁnyāsa` (the mere renunciation of action).

Why is Karma Yoga preferred? Krishna is not advocating for inaction. He has consistently taught that one cannot simply cease to act as long as one is embodied and subject to the material modes (gunas). True renunciation, as he will explain later, is an internal state of detachment from the fruits of action, not the abandonment of action itself. Mere physical renunciation, without inner purity and detachment, can lead to hypocrisy or a state of mental agitation, as the senses and mind might still crave worldly objects, even if the body is inactive.

Karma Yoga, on the other hand, provides a practical and accessible path for most individuals. It allows one to purify the mind and senses *through* engagement in the world, by performing duties with a spirit of service and without attachment to the outcomes. It transforms ordinary work into a spiritual practice. This path is less prone to misinterpretation and is more suitable for those who are not yet fully detached from worldly desires. It helps in gradual purification and self-mastery, making the mind steady and ready for higher spiritual realizations.

This verse reinforces the idea that the internal attitude is paramount. A karma yogi, while actively engaged in the world, cultivates an inner sannyasa – a renunciation of desire and attachment. This 'action in inaction' (as described in Chapter 4) is what makes karma yoga a more robust and generally superior path for the majority of spiritual aspirants.

### Practical Application
1.  **Transforming Daily Work**: Instead of viewing your job, household chores, or responsibilities as burdens or solely for personal gain, approach them as opportunities for service and contribution. Focus on the effort and the quality of your work, rather than being overly anxious about promotions, recognition, or specific outcomes. This detachment reduces stress and fosters inner peace.
2.  **Handling Success and Failure**: In both professional and personal life, one inevitably encounters successes and failures. A karma yogi maintains equanimity. Celebrate successes without excessive pride and face failures without deep despair. Understand that both are temporary and offer lessons. Your internal state of peace and commitment to duty remains undisturbed.
3.  **Mindful Engagement**: Whether you're interacting with family, colleagues, or strangers, engage fully and with kindness, but without expecting a particular response or outcome. For instance, offer help without expecting gratitude, or listen empathetically without expecting your advice to be followed. This practice cultivates genuine compassion and reduces emotional entanglement.

## Key Concepts
*   **Karma Yoga**: The path of selfless action performed without attachment to results.
*   **Sannyasa**: Renunciation, especially the internal detachment from the fruits of action.
*   **Niḥśreyasa**: The supreme good, ultimate liberation, or salvation.
*   **Internal vs. External Renunciation**: The distinction between outwardly abandoning action and inwardly detaching from desire and attachment while still acting.
