## Explanation

### Context
Chapter 5 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled 'Karma Sanyasa Yoga,' delves into the intricate relationship between renunciation of action (Sankhya) and performance of action without attachment (Karma Yoga). Arjuna's initial confusion, expressed in verse 1 of this chapter, pits these two paths against each other. Krishna, however, systematically clarifies that they are not contradictory but rather complementary, leading to the same ultimate spiritual realization. Verse 5.5 is a pivotal statement in this clarification, serving as a powerful synthesis. After establishing that both renunciation and performance of action can be liberating if approached with the right understanding (verse 2), and defining the true renunciate (verse 3), Krishna directly addresses the common destination of these seemingly distinct paths in this verse. He emphasizes that the 'goal' is singular, irrespective of the 'method' employed, provided the method is correctly understood and practiced.

### Word-by-Word Meaning
*   **यत्तत् (yat-tat)**: That which; referring to the supreme state of liberation or Brahman.
*   **सांख्यैः (sāṅkhyaiḥ)**: By the Sāṅkhyas; followers of the path of knowledge, intellectual discernment, and renunciation of action (Jnana Yoga or Sanyasa).
*   **प्राप्यते (prāpyate)**: Is attained; reached or achieved.
*   **तद्योगैः (tad yogaiḥ)**: That by the Yogis; followers of the path of action, selfless service, and disciplined effort (Karma Yoga).
*   **अपि (api)**: Also; indicating an equivalence.
*   **गम्यते (gamyate)**: Is reached; arrived at.
*   **एकं (ekaṁ)**: One; singular, unified.
*   **सांख्यं च योगं च (sāṅkhyaṁ ca yogaṁ ca)**: Both Sāṅkhya and Yoga; emphasizing the two paths Krishna has been discussing.
*   **यः पश्यति (yaḥ paśyati)**: He who sees; one who perceives or understands.
*   **स पश्यति (sa paśyati)**: He truly sees; implying profound insight and correct understanding.

### Deeper Understanding
Krishna's teaching in this verse is profound, cutting through superficial distinctions that often divide spiritual aspirants. He is not saying that Sankhya and Yoga are *identical* in their external practices, but that their *result* and the *inner state* they cultivate are ultimately the same. The 'state' referred to is moksha, liberation from the cycle of birth and death, or the realization of the ultimate truth (Brahman). 

For the Sankhya practitioner, this state is attained through discriminative knowledge, understanding the distinction between the eternal Self (Purusha) and transient matter (Prakriti), leading to renunciation of identification with actions and their results. For the Yogi (Karma Yogi), this state is reached through selfless action, performing duties without attachment to the fruits, offering all actions to the Divine, and maintaining equanimity in success and failure. 

What unites them is the *inner renunciation* – the detachment from the ego's desires and the identification with the transient world. A true Sankhya renunciate is internally detached, even if physically inactive. A true Karma Yogi, while outwardly active, is also internally detached, performing actions as an offering, not for personal gain. Both ultimately arrive at a state of peace, freedom, and union with the Divine. The key concept here is 'true vision' (sa paśyati), which is the ability to perceive this underlying unity, to look beyond the form and grasp the essence. This relates directly to dharma, as both paths, when correctly followed, align the individual with their true nature and purpose, leading to ultimate well-being.

### Practical Application
This verse offers immense practical wisdom for contemporary life:

1.  **Transcending Methodolatry**: In today's diverse spiritual landscape, people often get caught up in debating which path is 'better' – meditation vs. service, devotion vs. knowledge, etc. This verse encourages us to look beyond the methods and focus on the ultimate goal: inner peace, self-realization, and selfless living. Whether one finds solace in quiet contemplation or active community service, the crucial element is the cultivation of a detached and loving attitude.
2.  **Unity in Professional Life**: Consider two individuals in the workplace: one, a deep researcher or strategist (akin to Sankhya, seeking knowledge and understanding), and another, an operational manager or frontline worker (akin to Yoga, focused on action and execution). Both can contribute meaningfully and find fulfillment if they approach their work with dedication, detachment from outcomes, and a sense of service, rather than personal ego or gain. The 'true seer' recognizes the value and common underlying purpose in both roles.
3.  **Interfaith Harmony**: The principle extends beyond individual spiritual paths to broader religious and philosophical traditions. While rituals, doctrines, and practices may differ vastly across religions, the core ethical values, the pursuit of truth, and the aspiration for a higher state of being often converge. Recognizing this fundamental unity, as Krishna suggests, fosters tolerance, understanding, and harmony in a pluralistic world.

## Key Concepts

*   **Unity of Paths**: The ultimate goal achieved by both the path of knowledge (Sankhya) and the path of action (Karma Yoga) is the same.
*   **True Vision (Sa Pashyati)**: The profound insight to recognize the non-duality and common destination of diverse spiritual approaches.
*   **Inner Renunciation**: The critical common factor where detachment from desires and results, rather than mere external action or inaction, defines the success of either path.
*   **Goal of Liberation**: Both Sankhya and Yoga, when practiced correctly, lead to moksha or freedom from suffering and identification with the material world.
