The Yogi's Supreme Bliss: Tranquility and Brahman Realization (BG 6.27)

This verse describes the profound peace and supreme happiness attained by a yogi whose mind is tranquil, passions subdued, and who realizes Brahman.

October 2, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Atma Samyama Yoga
6.27

Chapter 6: Atma Samyama Yoga

Verse 27

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

प्रशान्तमनसं ह्येनं योगिनं सुखमुत्तमम्। उपैति शान्तरजसं ब्रह्मभूतमकल्मषम्॥

Transliteration (IAST)

praśāntamanasaṁ hyenaṁ yoginaṁ sukhamuttamam | upaiti śāntarajasaṁ brahmabhūtamakalmaṣam ||

Translation

Indeed, supreme happiness comes to this yogi whose mind is perfectly tranquil, whose passion is pacified, who is free from impurities, and who has become one with Brahman.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 6 of the Bhagavad Gita, known as Dhyana Yoga or Atma Samyama Yoga, meticulously outlines the path of meditation and self-control. Arjuna, initially skeptical about controlling the restless mind, is guided by Krishna through various techniques and disciplines required for a successful yogi. This particular verse (6.27) stands as a profound declaration of the ultimate reward and state achieved by such a dedicated practitioner. Having discussed the process of withdrawal, concentration, and stabilization of the mind in previous verses, Krishna now describes the blissful outcome for the yogi who truly masters these practices, revealing the characteristics and benefits of a fully realized state.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • प्रशान्तमनसम् (praśānta-manasam): ‘Praśānta’ means perfectly tranquil, calm, peaceful. ‘Manasam’ refers to the mind. So, ‘whose mind is perfectly tranquil.’ This indicates a mind free from agitation, worries, and distractions.
  • हि (hi): Indeed, certainly. An emphatic particle, assuring the truth of the statement.
  • एनम् (enam): This. Referring to the yogi described.
  • योगिनम् (yoginam): Yogi, one who practices yoga, a spiritual aspirant.
  • सुखमुत्तमम् (sukham uttamam): ‘Sukham’ is happiness, bliss. ‘Uttamam’ means supreme, highest. Thus, ‘supreme happiness’ or ‘highest bliss.’
  • उपैति (upaiti): Attains, approaches, comes to.
  • शान्तरजसम् (śānta-rajasam): ‘Śānta’ means pacified, calmed. ‘Rajas’ refers to the quality of passion, activity, desire, and restlessness, one of the three Gunas (Sattva, Rajas, Tamas). So, ‘whose passion is pacified’ or ‘whose quality of restlessness has subsided.’
  • ब्रह्मभूतम् (brahma-bhūtam): ‘Brahma’ refers to the Absolute Truth, the ultimate reality. ‘Bhūtam’ means having become, being. Therefore, ‘one who has become one with Brahman’ or ‘one who has realized Brahman.’ This signifies a state of spiritual oneness with the divine ground of existence.
  • अकल्मषम् (akalmaṣam): ‘A’ means without, ‘kalmaṣam’ means sin, impurity, fault. So, ‘free from impurities’ or ‘spotless.’ This implies moral purity and freedom from the karmic residues of past actions.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna here outlines the defining characteristics of a truly successful yogi and the unparalleled reward they experience. The core teaching is that true, lasting happiness (sukham uttamam) is not found in external pursuits but arises from an internal transformation.

  1. Mental Tranquility (Praśānta Manasam): The foundation of this state is a mind that is utterly calm. In a world of constant sensory input and mental chatter, achieving praśānta manasam is a monumental task, yet it is the prerequisite for deeper spiritual experience. This tranquility is not mere absence of noise, but a deep inner stillness where the mind no longer creates its own disturbances.

  2. Pacification of Passion (Śānta Rajasam): The Guna of Rajas drives desire, ambition, attachment, and restlessness. It is the source of much human suffering, as desires, whether fulfilled or unfulfilled, keep the mind agitated. By pacifying rajas, the yogi transcends the cycle of wanting and aversion, leading to a state of contentment and inner freedom. This doesn’t mean becoming inactive, but acting without attachment to the fruits of action, driven by a higher purpose rather than selfish desire.

  3. Freedom from Impurities (Akalmaṣam): This refers to both moral purity and the cleansing of karmic impressions. A yogi committed to this path lives a virtuous life, shedding ego, anger, greed, and other vices. This ethical foundation is crucial, as inner impurities cloud perception and prevent the realization of the Self. Freedom from kalmaṣam also implies transcending the dualities of good and evil, moving towards a state of pure consciousness.

  4. Brahman Realization (Brahma-bhūtam): This is the pinnacle of the yogi’s journey. It signifies the realization that one’s individual Self (Atman) is identical with the universal Self (Brahman). It’s a profound experience of oneness with all existence, where the illusion of separation dissolves. This state is not just intellectual understanding but a direct, experiential knowing that transcends the limitations of the individual ego. It relates deeply to the theme of yoga as union – the union of the individual consciousness with the Cosmic Consciousness.

These characteristics cumulatively lead to sukham uttamam, supreme happiness, which is distinct from fleeting worldly pleasures. This is an abiding, unshakeable bliss that is independent of external circumstances, a state of profound peace and joy inherent in one’s true nature.

Practical Application

This verse offers invaluable guidance for modern life, where stress, anxiety, and the pursuit of external gratification are rampant:

  1. Cultivating Inner Stillness: In a fast-paced world, dedicate time daily to practices that calm the mind. This could be meditation, mindful breathing, or simply spending moments in quiet contemplation. Even five minutes of focused silence can begin to pacify the mind (praśānta manasam) and reduce the dominance of rajas.
  2. Mindful Desire Management: Become aware of the desires that drive your actions. Are they truly necessary, or are they fueling restlessness? Practicing non-attachment (not indifference, but acting without craving for specific outcomes) in work and relationships can significantly reduce mental agitation and help in becoming śānta rajasam.
  3. Ethical Living and Self-Reflection: Regularly assess your actions, thoughts, and words. Are they aligned with compassion, truthfulness, and kindness? Consciously working to eliminate negative habits and cultivate virtues helps in becoming akalmaṣam, purifying the mind and heart, and laying the groundwork for deeper spiritual insights.

Reflection

  • How often do you experience true mental tranquility in your daily life? What are the primary obstacles to it?
  • Can you identify instances where your desires (rajas) have led to agitation or unhappiness? How might you approach them differently?
  • What steps can you take to cultivate greater inner purity and a sense of oneness with the world around you?

Key Concepts

  • Mental Tranquility (Praśānta Manasam): The state of a perfectly calm and undisturbed mind, free from agitation.
  • Pacification of Passion (Śānta Rajasam): The subsidence of the quality of restlessness, desire, and attachment, leading to inner peace.
  • Brahman Realization (Brahma-bhūtam): The direct, experiential understanding of the ultimate reality and one’s oneness with it.
  • Supreme Happiness (Sukham Uttamam): An unshakeable, inherent bliss that transcends external circumstances and worldly pleasures.
