The Mark of Steady Wisdom: Desirelessness and Self-Contentment (Bhagavad Gita 2.55)

Krishna defines the sthitaprajña as one who renounces all mental desires and finds complete satisfaction within the Self.

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 55

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

श्रीभगवानुवाच प्रजहाति यदा कामान्सर्वान्पार्थ मनोगतान्। आत्मन्येवात्मना तुष्टः स्थितप्रज्ञस्तदोच्यते॥ ५५॥

Transliteration (IAST)

śrībhagavānuvāca prajahāti yadā kāmān sarvān pārtha manogatān | ātmanyevātmanā tuṣṭaḥ sthitaprajñastadocyate || 55 ||

Translation

The Supreme Lord said: When a man completely renounces all desires that arise in the mind, O Pārtha, and is satisfied in the Self alone by the Self, then he is called one of steady wisdom.

Explanation

Context

This profound verse marks the commencement of Lord Krishna’s direct answer to Arjuna’s crucial question posed in verse 54: “What are the characteristics of a person of steady wisdom (sthitaprajña)? How does he speak, sit, and walk?” Arjuna, overwhelmed by the moral dilemma of war, seeks clarity on the nature of a truly enlightened being—one whose intellect (prajña) is firmly established (sthita) in truth. Krishna begins by laying the foundational characteristic: the transcendence of desire and the realization of inner contentment. This definition sets the stage for the subsequent verses, which further elaborate on the conduct and mental state of such a liberated soul, providing a comprehensive blueprint for spiritual realization within the framework of Sankhya Yoga.

Word-by-Word Meaning

Let’s unpack the key terms to grasp the depth of this teaching:

  • प्रजहाति (prajahāti): This is a powerful verb meaning ‘completely gives up,’ ‘abandon,’ or ‘renounces.’ The prefix ‘pra-’ intensifies the action, implying a thorough and decisive relinquishment, not merely a suppression or temporary avoidance. It suggests a conscious, deliberate act of letting go.
  • यदा (yadā): ‘When.’ This indicates a condition or a point in time when this state is achieved.
  • कामान् (kāmān): ‘Desires,’ ‘cravings,’ ‘longings,’ ‘wishes.’ In the Gita, ‘kāma’ often refers to sensual desires, but here, qualified by ‘manogatān,’ it encompasses all forms of mental aspirations, attachments, and cravings that arise in the mind, whether for pleasure, power, recognition, or material possessions.
  • सर्वान् (sarvān): ‘All.’ This emphasizes the totality of the renunciation. It’s not about giving up some desires, but all of them that are ‘mind-born.’
  • पार्थ (pārtha): ‘O son of Pṛthā.’ This is a loving address by Krishna to Arjuna, signifying a personal and intimate teaching, emphasizing the importance of the message.
  • मनोगतान् (manogatān): ‘Residing in the mind,’ ‘born of the mind.’ This crucial qualifier highlights that the desires to be renounced are those that originate and dwell within the mental faculty. It’s not about rejecting the external world or action, but about detaching from the internal mental constructs of craving and attachment.
  • आत्मनि (ātmani): ‘In the Self,’ ‘within oneself.’ This refers to the true, eternal, unchanging Self (Atman), the spiritual core of one’s being, distinct from the temporary body, mind, and ego.
  • एव (eva): ‘Alone,’ ‘only,’ ‘exclusively.’ This adverb stresses that satisfaction is found solely within the Self, without any dependence on external objects or circumstances.
  • आत्मना (ātmanā): ‘By the Self,’ ‘through the Self,’ ‘by one’s own inner being.’ This signifies that the contentment is self-generated, arising from one’s own spiritual realization and inner experience, not from external stimuli.
  • तुष्टः (tuṣṭaḥ): ‘Satisfied,’ ‘contented,’ ‘fulfilled.’ This describes a state of profound inner peace and completeness.
  • स्थितप्रज्ञः (sthitaprajñaḥ): ‘One of steady wisdom,’ ‘stable intellect.’ This is the term Arjuna asked Krishna to define, representing the enlightened sage whose understanding of truth is unwavering.
  • तदा (tadā): ‘Then.’ Indicating the consequence of the prior condition.
  • उच्यते (ucyate): ‘Is called,’ ‘is said to be.‘

Deeper Understanding

Krishna is teaching Arjuna that true spiritual wisdom is not merely intellectual knowledge but a stable, unwavering state of consciousness, characterized by freedom from mental desires and complete inner contentment. This state, known as sthitaprajña, is achieved when an individual transcends the constant pull of kāma (desire) that originates in the mind (manogatān).

The key concepts presented here are:

  1. Renunciation of Mind-Born Desires (Kāma-tyāga): This isn’t about becoming an ascetic who shuns all worldly interactions. Rather, it’s an internal detachment from the craving for results and external objects. Desires, when they become attachments, bind the individual to the cycle of action and reaction, constantly seeking external validation or pleasure. By renouncing manogatān desires, one breaks free from this mental slavery. The emphasis is on the mind-born nature of these desires, implying that true freedom lies in mastering one’s internal landscape, not in changing external circumstances.

  2. Self-Contentment (Ātma-tuṣṭi): The sthitaprajña finds joy and fulfillment not in external achievements, possessions, or relationships, but within the Atman itself. This state of ātmanyevātmanā tuṣṭaḥ signifies an unshakeable inner peace and completeness. When satisfaction comes from within, one becomes immune to the fluctuating nature of the external world, which can bring both pleasure and pain. This inner sufficiency is the ultimate freedom from external dependency, a state of profound psychological and spiritual liberation.

This verse lays the philosophical cornerstone for several Gita themes:

  • Karma Yoga: If one is truly desireless and self-content, actions are performed without attachment to their results, becoming pure duty or offering, thus aligning with the principles of Karma Yoga.
  • Sankhya Yoga: The realization of the Atman as distinct from the mind and its desires is a core tenet of Sankhya philosophy. This verse defines the practical outcome of such discriminative knowledge.
  • Yoga of Equanimity: A person who is self-content and desireless is naturally equanimous, undisturbed by the dualities of life (pleasure/pain, gain/loss, honor/dishonor) because their inner state is independent of external fluctuations.
  • Moksha (Liberation): This state of desirelessness and self-contentment is a definitive hallmark of liberation from the cycle of birth and death, as it signifies freedom from the karmic impressions created by desire-driven actions.

Practical Application

The wisdom of this verse is highly relevant for modern life, offering a pathway to reduce stress and find inner peace:

  1. Mindful Observation of Desires: Instead of immediately pursuing every desire that arises, practice observing it. Ask: “Is this a true need, or a mind-born craving influenced by advertising, social comparison, or fleeting emotion?” This pause creates space to choose whether to engage or let go. For example, before making an impulse purchase, take a moment to reflect on whether it truly aligns with your values or if it’s just a momentary desire for gratification.
  2. Cultivating Inner Sources of Joy: Consciously shift focus from external achievements and possessions to internal well-being. Engage in practices like meditation, gratitude journaling, or spending time in nature, which cultivate a sense of inner peace and contentment. For instance, instead of constantly striving for the next promotion for happiness, find joy in the process of learning, contributing, and connecting with colleagues, irrespective of the outcome.
  3. Detachment in Action: Perform your duties and pursue your goals with diligence and dedication, but without being overly attached to the specific outcomes. Understand that true satisfaction comes from the effort and the integrity of the action itself, rather than the anticipated reward. For example, a student studies diligently for the sake of knowledge and personal growth, finding satisfaction in the learning process, rather than solely for the grade or external validation.

Reflection

  • How often do I allow my happiness to be dictated by external circumstances or the fulfillment of desires?
  • What does it truly mean for me to be “satisfied in the Self alone by the Self” in my daily experiences?
  • What steps can I take today to observe my desires more mindfully and cultivate a deeper sense of inner contentment?

Key Concepts

  1. Desirelessness (Kāma-tyāga): The complete and conscious renunciation of all desires that originate in the mind.
  2. Self-Contentment (Ātma-tuṣṭi): The profound state of finding joy, satisfaction, and fulfillment exclusively within one’s true, inner Self.
  3. Steady Wisdom (Sthitaprajña): An unwavering and stable intellect, characteristic of an enlightened individual whose consciousness is unperturbed by external dualities.
  4. Inner Freedom: The liberation from the constant pull of the mind’s cravings, leading to independence from external sources of happiness and suffering.
