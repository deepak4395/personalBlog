The Inner Renunciation: Dwelling in the Nine-Gated City

This verse explains how the self-controlled embodied soul, by mentally renouncing all actions, resides peacefully within the body, neither acting nor causing action.

August 20, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Sanyasa Yoga
5.13

Chapter 5: Karma Sanyasa Yoga

Verse 13

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

सर्वकर्माणि मनसा संन्यस्यास्ते सुखं वशी। नवद्वारे पुरे देही नैव कुर्वन्न कारयन्॥

Transliteration (IAST)

sarvakarmāṇi manasā saṁnyasyāste sukhaṁ vaśī। navadvāre pure dehī naiva kurvanna kārayan॥

Translation

The embodied soul, having mentally renounced all actions, rests happily as the self-controlled master in the nine-gated city (the body), neither acting nor causing action.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 5, ‘Karma Sanyasa Yoga’, delves into the intricate relationship between renunciation of action (sannyasa) and action in devotion (karma yoga). Arjuna, in previous chapters, was confused about which path was superior. Krishna clarifies that both paths, when understood correctly, lead to the same goal. This particular verse (5.13) builds upon the idea of true renunciation, moving beyond the external cessation of work to an internal, mental detachment. It emphasizes that a wise person, even while engaged in worldly activities, maintains an inner state of non-doership, understanding that the soul is distinct from the body and its actions. It sets the stage for a deeper understanding of the nature of the self and its interaction with the material world.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • सर्वकर्माणि (sarvakarmāṇi): All actions, referring to all physical, mental, and verbal activities performed by an individual.
  • मनसा (manasā): By the mind. This is crucial, highlighting that the renunciation is internal, not merely external.
  • संन्यस्य (saṁnyasya): Having renounced, having given up. This is not about giving up the act itself, but the attachment to the action and its results, and the false sense of being the sole doer.
  • आस्ते (āste): Remains, sits, dwells. Implies a state of being, a peaceful existence.
  • सुखं (sukhaṁ): Happily, at ease, peacefully. The result of such mental renunciation is inner contentment.
  • वशी (vaśī): The self-controlled one, master of oneself. This refers to someone who has gained mastery over their senses and mind.
  • नवद्वारे (navadvāre): In the nine-gated. A metaphorical description of the human body.
  • पुरे (pure): City. The body is likened to a city, a dwelling place.
  • देही (dehī): The embodied soul. The individual consciousness residing within the body.
  • न एव (na eva): Neither indeed.
  • कुर्वन् (kurvan): Doing, acting.
  • न (na): Nor.
  • कारयन् (kārayan): Causing to do, instigating action.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna here presents a profound teaching on the nature of the self and the illusion of doership. The core message is that true renunciation is not about abandoning one’s duties or withdrawing from society. Instead, it is an internal spiritual discipline where one mentally detaches from the outcomes of actions and the egoistic notion of being the sole doer. The dehī, the embodied soul, is inherently transcendental, a witness (sākṣī), distinct from the material body and mind (prakriti) that perform actions.

The body is described as a navadvāre pure – a nine-gated city. These nine gates are typically identified as the two eyes, two ears, two nostrils, the mouth, and the two organs of excretion and generation. This metaphor emphasizes that the body is merely a temporary dwelling, a vehicle, for the eternal soul. The dehī, as vaśī (self-controlled), is the master of this city, not its prisoner. It consciously chooses not to identify with the sensory inputs and outputs, remaining aloof and unattached.

The phrase naiva kurvan na kārayan – ‘neither acting nor causing action’ – is central to the philosophy of non-doership (akartābhāva). It does not mean physical inactivity. Rather, it signifies that the pure consciousness (ātman) is not the actual performer of actions. All actions are performed by the three modes of material nature (gunas) operating through the body, mind, and senses. The wise person understands this distinction, thus remaining unaffected by the karmic reactions of actions performed by the body. This understanding liberates one from the entanglement of karma, allowing them to live sukhaṁ – happily and peacefully – even amidst the hustle of life. This relates directly to the themes of Karma Yoga, where actions are performed as an offering, without attachment to results, and Sannyasa, which is the internal renunciation of ego and attachment.

Practical Application

  1. Mindful Detachment in Work: Approach your daily tasks, professional or personal, with dedication and skill, but consciously release attachment to the outcomes. For instance, if you’re working on a project, give your best effort, but understand that success or failure is influenced by many factors beyond your sole control. This reduces stress and anxiety.
  2. Observing Thoughts and Emotions: Practice mindfulness to observe your thoughts and emotions without immediately identifying with them. Recognize that you are the observer (dehī), not merely the fleeting thoughts or emotions. This creates an inner space, allowing for calmer responses rather than reactive ones.
  3. Cultivating Inner Peace Amidst Chaos: In a world filled with constant demands and external stimuli, consciously retreat to your ‘inner city’ (your true self). By understanding that the external actions and their results do not define your core being, you can maintain a sense of sukhaṁ (happiness and peace) even when external circumstances are turbulent. This empowers you to act from a place of clarity rather than compulsion.

Reflection

  • How often do I feel overwhelmed by the results of my actions, and how can I practice mental renunciation in those moments?
  • In what ways do I identify too strongly with my body, mind, or external roles, forgetting my true nature as the dehī?
  • What does it mean for me to be vaśī – a self-controlled master – in the ‘nine-gated city’ of my body?

Key Concepts

  • Mental Renunciation (Manasa Sannyasa): The internal act of giving up attachment to actions and their fruits, rather than physical cessation of work.
  • Non-doership (Akartābhāva): The understanding that the pure soul is not the actual performer of actions; actions are performed by material nature.
  • The Nine-Gated City (Navadvāre Pure): A metaphor for the human body, emphasizing its role as a temporary dwelling for the soul.
  • Self-Control (Vaśī): Mastery over one’s senses and mind, enabling one to remain undisturbed by external circumstances.
