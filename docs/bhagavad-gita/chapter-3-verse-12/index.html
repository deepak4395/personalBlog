The Cosmic Exchange: Are We Giving Back Enough?

Krishna explains that divine forces bestow gifts upon us, and those who enjoy them without reciprocation are indeed thieves.

May 27, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Yoga #Reciprocity #Yajña #Dharma #Gratitude #Selfless Action
3.12

Chapter 3: Karma Yoga

Verse 12

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

इष्टान्भोगान्हि वो देवा दास्यन्ते यज्ञभाविताः। तैर्दत्तानप्रदायैभ्यो यो भुङ्क्ते स्तेन एव सः॥१२॥

Transliteration (IAST)

iṣṭānbhogānhiva vo devā dāsyante yajñabhāvitāḥ | tairdattānapradāyaibhyo yo bhuṅkte stena eva saḥ || 12 ||

Translation

The gods, nourished by sacrifice, will indeed bestow upon you all the desired enjoyments. He who enjoys these gifts without offering anything in return to them is verily a thief.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 3 of the Bhagavad Gita, ‘Karma Yoga,’ delves into the necessity and nature of action in the world, particularly in light of Arjuna’s confusion about whether action or renunciation is superior. Lord Krishna has just introduced the concept of yajña (sacrifice or selfless action) as the very foundation of creation and sustenance. In verse 10, He declared that Prajapati (the Creator) created humanity along with yajña, instructing them to prosper through it. Verse 11 elaborated, stating that by nourishing the devas (divine beings or natural forces) through yajña, they, in turn, nourish humanity, leading to mutual prosperity. This twelfth verse further solidifies this divine contract, warning against the grave error of consuming without contributing, framing it as a transgression against the cosmic order.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • इष्टान् (iṣṭān): desired, cherished, longed-for.
  • भोगान् (bhogān): enjoyments, pleasures, objects of experience.
  • हि (hi): indeed, certainly, surely – emphasizing the truth of the statement.
  • वः (vaḥ): to you (plural).
  • देवाः (devāḥ): gods, divine beings, personifications of natural forces (like the sun, rain, earth, air) that govern the universe.
  • दास्यन्ते (dāsyante): will give, will bestow, will grant.
  • यज्ञभाविताः (yajñabhāvitāḥ): nourished by sacrifice, pleased by sacrifice, sustained or enhanced by yajña. This implies that the devas are not just givers but also receive sustenance from human efforts.
  • तैः (taiḥ): by them (the devas).
  • दत्तान् (dattān): given, bestowed, granted.
  • अप्रदाय (apradāya): without giving in return, without offering, without reciprocating.
  • एभ्यः (ebhyaḥ): to them (the devas).
  • यः (yaḥ): who, he who.
  • भुङ्क्ते (bhuṅkte): enjoys, consumes, partakes of.
  • स्तेनः (stenaḥ): a thief, one who steals.
  • एव (eva): indeed, verily, certainly – reinforcing the strong condemnation.
  • सः (saḥ): he.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s teaching here transcends mere ritualistic offerings. When He speaks of ‘gods’ (devas), He is referring to the intricate network of universal energies and natural forces that sustain life. The sun provides light, rain nourishes crops, the earth yields food, and air allows us to breathe. These are the ‘desired enjoyments’ bestowed upon us. The concept of yajña (sacrifice) is thus broadened to encompass any action performed with a spirit of gratitude, service, and contribution to the greater good, thereby maintaining cosmic balance.

The verse highlights a fundamental principle of existence: reciprocity. Life is a continuous cycle of giving and receiving. We receive countless blessings—from the natural world, from our communities, from the wisdom of ancestors, and from the labor of others. To merely consume these gifts without contributing back is deemed a form of theft. It’s not just about taking material possessions but about taking without acknowledging the source, without contributing to the cycle that makes such gifts possible. This self-centered approach disrupts the natural order (dharma) and accrues negative karma.

Krishna’s strong language—calling such a person a ‘thief’—underscores the seriousness of this imbalance. It’s a powerful moral and spiritual indictment against a life of pure consumption and self-gratification without any sense of duty or contribution. This teaching is central to Karma Yoga, emphasizing that right action (karma) must be performed with an awareness of our interconnectedness and an attitude of selfless service, contributing to the welfare of all, rather than solely for personal gain.

Practical Application

This ancient wisdom holds profound relevance for modern life. It encourages a shift from a purely consumerist mindset to one of mindful contribution and gratitude.

  1. Environmental Stewardship: Recognizing that clean air, water, fertile land, and biodiversity are ‘gifts’ from nature (the devas), we have a responsibility to protect and replenish them. Practical applications include reducing our carbon footprint, recycling, conserving resources, supporting sustainable agriculture, and advocating for environmental protection rather than merely exploiting natural resources for personal comfort.
  2. Community and Social Responsibility: We benefit immensely from the infrastructure, peace, education, and services provided by our communities and society. Reciprocating means actively participating in civic life, volunteering, paying taxes honestly, supporting local initiatives, and contributing our skills and resources to uplift others. It’s about not just taking from society but also building and maintaining it.
  3. Mindful Consumption and Gratitude: Before consuming food, water, or any resource, pausing to acknowledge the farmers, laborers, and natural processes involved in bringing it to us. This cultivates gratitude and discourages wasteful habits. For instance, reducing food waste or buying ethically sourced products can be seen as modern forms of yajña—conscious choices that honor the sources of our sustenance.

Reflection

  • What ‘gifts’ do I receive daily from nature, my community, and the efforts of others that I often take for granted?
  • How consciously do I reciprocate these gifts through my actions and contributions?
  • Am I living a life primarily of consumption, or am I actively participating in the cycle of giving and receiving?
  • What specific actions can I take to embody the spirit of yajña and avoid being a ‘thief’ of cosmic resources?

Key Concepts

  • Reciprocity: The fundamental principle that life is a cycle of mutual giving and receiving.
  • Yajña (Selfless Action): Broadened beyond ritual to encompass all actions performed with a spirit of service and contribution to the cosmic order.
  • Dharma (Righteous Conduct): The moral and ethical obligation to contribute to the sustenance of the world from which we receive.
  • Cosmic Interconnectedness: The understanding that all beings and forces are linked, and individual actions have far-reaching consequences for the whole.
