The Mind and Wandering Senses: A Ship Without a Rudder

This verse vividly illustrates how an uncontrolled mind, swayed by senses, can lead to the loss of discrimination, akin to a boat adrift in a storm.

May 10, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga
2.67

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 67

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

इन्द्रियाणां हि चरतां यन्मनोऽनुविधीयते। तदस्य हरति प्रज्ञां वायुर्नावमिवाम्भसि॥

Transliteration (IAST)

indriyāṇāṁ hi caratāṁ yanmano'nuvidhīyate | tadasya harati prajñāṁ vāyurnāvamivāmbhasi ||

Translation

For the mind, when it yields to the wandering senses, carries away one's discrimination, just as a strong wind carries away a boat on the water.

Explanation

Context

This profound verse (2.67) is a crucial part of Krishna’s discourse on the characteristics of a sthita-prajña, a person of steady wisdom, in Bhagavad Gita Chapter 2, Sankhya Yoga. Having previously described the ideal state of mental equanimity and the necessity of sense control, Krishna now vividly illustrates the peril of losing that control. The preceding verses (2.62-63) outlined a downward spiral from attachment to ruin, while verse 2.64 hinted at the path to tranquility through regulated senses. Verse 2.66 explicitly stated that an uncontrolled mind cannot find peace or happiness. Building upon this, verse 2.67 provides a powerful analogy to explain how this loss of control occurs and its immediate, detrimental effect on one’s inherent wisdom and discernment. It serves as a stark warning, emphasizing the fragile nature of prajña (discrimination) when the mind is not disciplined.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • इन्द्रियाणाम् (indriyāṇām): Of the senses. This refers to the five organs of perception (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch) and the five organs of action (speech, grasping, locomotion, excretion, procreation).
  • हि (hi): Indeed, truly, certainly. It adds emphasis.
  • चरताम् (caratām): Wandering, moving about, active, engrossed. It implies senses that are unrestrained and seeking external objects.
  • यत् मनः (yat manaḥ): That mind which. The mind is the central processing unit, the seat of thoughts, emotions, and desires.
  • अनुविधीयते (anuvidhīyate): Follows, yields to, conforms to, is led by. This highlights the mind’s choice to surrender its autonomy and passively trail the senses.
  • तत् (tat): That (mind).
  • अस्य (asya): Of this (person). Refers to the individual whose mind is uncontrolled.
  • हरति (harati): Carries away, seizes, takes away, destroys, steals. Implies a forceful and detrimental removal.
  • प्रज्ञाम् (prajñām): Discrimination, wisdom, intellect, discerning knowledge. It is the faculty that distinguishes right from wrong, real from unreal, and beneficial from harmful.
  • वायुः (vāyuḥ): Wind, a strong gust of wind.
  • नावम् (nāvam): Boat, ship, vessel.
  • इव (iva): Like, as, similar to.
  • अम्भसि (ambhasi): In the water, on the ocean.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s teaching here is profoundly insightful into human psychology. He isn’t saying the senses themselves are inherently bad; rather, it’s the mind’s uncontrolled yielding to them that becomes problematic. The senses are naturally drawn to their objects (eyes to sights, ears to sounds, etc.). If the mind lacks discipline and simply follows these sensory impulses without discernment, it loses its capacity for prajña.

The analogy of the boat and the wind is exceptionally powerful and enduring. The ‘boat’ represents our intellect or power of discrimination (prajña), which ideally should navigate the waters of life (the world of experiences) with purpose and direction. The ‘strong wind’ symbolizes the mind, when it is swept away by the ‘wandering senses’ (the currents and waves of sensory desires and external stimuli). Just as a boat without a rudder, an anchor, or a skilled helmsman is tossed about and eventually wrecked by a storm, a person whose mind is not disciplined will lose their power of judgment, make impulsive decisions, and drift away from their higher purpose and well-being.

This verse underscores the mind’s pivotal role as the bridge between the inner self and the outer world. If this bridge is weak and easily swayed, the inner light of wisdom is extinguished. This directly relates to dharma (righteous conduct) because without prajña, one cannot make dharmic choices or uphold ethical principles. It connects to yoga as the very practice of chitta vritti nirodha – the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind – which is essential for controlling the senses. It also highlights the importance of vairagya (non-attachment) and abhyasa (persistent practice) in training the mind to be steadfast rather than a slave to fleeting desires. The verse serves as a critical warning against the subtle yet powerful way sense objects can hijack our inner state, leading to actions that are not aligned with our true self or long-term spiritual evolution.

Practical Application

  1. Mindful Digital Consumption: In our modern world, the ‘wandering senses’ are constantly bombarded by digital stimuli – social media feeds, endless news cycles, streaming content. If our mind passively follows these inputs, our ability to focus, think critically, and discern truth from noise diminishes. Practice ‘digital detoxes’ or set specific times for engaging with technology to prevent the mind from being constantly carried away.
  2. Conscious Eating and Spending: Many of our daily actions, like eating or shopping, are often driven by sensory gratification rather than genuine need or well-being. Before indulging in an impulse purchase or an unhealthy snack, pause and ask: “Is my mind being led by a fleeting desire, or is this a conscious choice aligned with my values and health?” This pause strengthens prajña.
  3. Cultivating Inner Stillness: Regular practice of meditation, mindfulness, or contemplative prayer helps develop a stronger ‘rudder’ for the mind. These practices train us to observe thoughts and sensory inputs without immediately reacting or being swept away. This strengthens our capacity to choose our responses rather than being dictated by external forces, thereby protecting and enhancing our discrimination.

Reflection

  • What are the primary ‘strong winds’ in my daily life that tend to carry my mind away from its intended course?
  • How often do I find myself making decisions based on immediate sensory gratification rather than thoughtful discrimination?
  • What deliberate steps can I take to cultivate a stronger ‘rudder’ for my mind and protect my inner wisdom from being swept away?

Key Concepts

  • Mind’s Sovereignty: The mind is not a passive recipient but an active agent that chooses whether to lead or be led by the senses.
  • Importance of Discrimination (Prajña): Our ability to discern, judge, and make wise choices is fragile and can be easily lost if the mind is uncontrolled.
  • Analogy of Boat and Wind: A powerful metaphor illustrating how an unanchored mind, swayed by senses, can lead to the loss of one’s guiding wisdom.
  • Path to Spiritual Stability: True peace and wisdom require disciplined control over the mind and senses, preventing them from dictating one’s inner state and actions.
