Desire's Veil: Why We Turn to Other Deities

Krishna explains that those whose wisdom is clouded by desires often worship other deities, driven by their inherent nature and specific rituals.

Chapter 7: Jnana Vijnana Yoga

Verse 20

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

कामैस्तैस्तैर्हृतज्ञानाः प्रपद्यन्तेऽन्यदेवताः। तं तं नियममास्थाय प्रकृत्या नियताः स्वया॥

Transliteration (IAST)

kāmaistai-stair hṛtajñānāḥ prapadyante'nya-devatāḥ | taṁ taṁ niyamam āsthāya prakṛtyā niyatāḥ svayā ||

Translation

Those whose wisdom has been carried away by various desires surrender to other deities, observing various specific rules, compelled by their own nature.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 7, “Jnana Vijnana Yoga,” is where Krishna reveals His supreme nature as the ultimate reality, the cause of all causes, and the essence of everything. He has just described Himself as the underlying principle of all existence – the taste in water, the light in the sun and moon, the sound in ether, the virility in men, the pure fragrance of the earth, the brilliance in fire, the life in all beings, and the austerity in ascetics (7.8-9). He also stated that He is the eternal seed of all beings, the intelligence of the intelligent, and the prowess of the powerful (7.10). He concludes that all states of being – whether sattvic, rajasic, or tamasic – originate from Him, yet He is transcendental to them (7.12).

Following this grand exposition of His divinity, Krishna begins to explain why, despite His omnipresence and omnipotence, not everyone recognizes or worships Him as the Supreme. He mentions in 7.15 that deluded persons (mūḍhāḥ) with demoniac nature (āsurīṁ bhāvam āśritāḥ) do not surrender to Him. Verse 7.16 then categorizes four kinds of pious people who worship Him: the distressed, the seekers of knowledge, the seekers of wealth, and the wise.

Verse 7.20 serves as a crucial bridge, explaining one of the primary reasons why individuals, though seeking some form of divine intervention, might not turn to Krishna as the Supreme Lord. It posits that material desires cloud their judgment, leading them to worship other deities or lesser manifestations, driven by their inherent nature and specific ritualistic observances. It sets the stage for Krishna to further elaborate on the nature of these “other gods” and the temporary results obtained from such worship, contrasting it with the eternal benefits of devotion to Him.

Word-by-Word Meaning

Let’s break down the key terms in this profound verse:

  • कामैः (kāmaiḥ): “by desires.” This refers to various material longings, aspirations for worldly gains, pleasures, or relief from suffering.
  • तैः तैः (taiḥ taiḥ): “by those various (desires).” The repetition emphasizes the multiplicity and diverse nature of these desires.
  • हृतज्ञानाः (hṛtajñānāḥ): “whose wisdom/knowledge has been stolen away.” Hṛta means ‘stolen’ or ‘carried away,’ and jñāna means ‘knowledge’ or ‘discriminative wisdom.’ This is a critical phrase, indicating that desires act like thieves, robbing one’s ability to discern the ultimate truth and the true source of fulfillment.
  • प्रपद्यन्ते (prapadyante): “they surrender,” “they resort to,” “they take refuge in.” This signifies an act of devotion or seeking protection/help.
  • अन्यदेवताः (anya-devatāḥ): “other deities/gods.” This refers to specific divine forms, demigods, or even powerful natural forces, often associated with granting particular boons, rather than the singular, all-encompassing Supreme Being.
  • तं तं नियमम् आस्थाय (taṁ taṁ niyamam āsthāya): “observing those various specific rules/rituals.” Niyamam means ‘rule’ or ‘observance,’ and āsthāya means ‘adopting’ or ‘observing.’ This highlights the practice of specific rites and rituals tailored to invoke particular deities for specific outcomes.
  • प्रकृत्या नियताः स्वया (prakṛtyā niyatāḥ svayā): “compelled by their own nature.” Prakṛti refers to one’s inherent nature, tendencies, or conditioning, largely influenced by the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas). Niyatāḥ means ‘bound’ or ‘controlled,’ and svayā means ‘by their own.’ This emphasizes that internal conditioning plays a significant role in shaping one’s desires and chosen path of worship.

Deeper Understanding

This verse offers a profound insight into human motivation and the dynamics of spiritual seeking. Krishna is not condemning the worship of other deities outright, but rather explaining the underlying reason why individuals might choose such paths instead of directly approaching the Supreme Lord.

  1. The Veil of Desire (Hṛtajñānāḥ): The central theme is the blinding power of desire. When the intellect (buddhi) is clouded by various material longings – for wealth, power, fame, progeny, health, or even liberation from immediate suffering – the discriminative faculty (jñāna) is compromised. Instead of seeking the ultimate, unconditional source of all blessings, individuals become preoccupied with specific, temporary outcomes. They fail to see that all energies, all powers, and all deities ultimately emanate from and are sustained by the Supreme Lord Himself. Their wisdom is “stolen” in the sense that they lose sight of the bigger picture, focusing on the branches rather than the root.

  2. Conditional Worship (Anya-devatāḥ, Niyamam Āsthāya): The worship of “other deities” in this context often implies a transactional relationship. People approach specific divine forms with specific requests, believing that these deities are specialized in granting particular boons. This is often accompanied by strict adherence to particular rituals (niyamam āsthāya), which are believed to invoke the desired results. This contrasts sharply with unconditional, selfless devotion (bhakti-yoga) to the Supreme Lord, where the devotee seeks nothing but the Lord’s grace and connection, understanding that all true blessings flow from that supreme source. Such conditional worship, while potentially yielding temporary results, does not lead to ultimate liberation (moksha) or self-realization.

  3. Nature’s Compulsion (Prakṛtyā Niyatāḥ Svayā): A crucial element is the influence of one’s own inherent nature (svayā prakṛtyā). Our conditioning, built up over countless lifetimes through karma and the interaction of the gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas), shapes our desires and our preferred mode of worship.

    • A person predominantly influenced by Rajas (passion, activity) might seek power, fame, or material abundance, and would worship deities associated with such boons.
    • A person predominantly influenced by Tamas (ignorance, inertia) might seek relief from suffering, engage in superstitious practices, or be drawn to darker forms of worship out of fear or delusion.
    • Even those influenced by Sattva (goodness, purity) might initially seek knowledge or spiritual elevation from specific deities before realizing the ultimate, all-encompassing nature of the Supreme. This implies that our spiritual journey is deeply intertwined with our psychological and karmic makeup. We are, to a significant extent, driven by these ingrained tendencies until we consciously work to transcend them.

This verse relates to various Gita themes:

  • Karma Yoga: Actions (karma) performed with desire for specific results (kāmaiḥ) lead to further entanglement. Actions performed with selfless devotion lead to liberation.
  • Jnana Yoga: The verse highlights the loss of jñāna (knowledge/wisdom) due to desire, underscoring the importance of discriminative intelligence to perceive the ultimate truth.
  • Bhakti Yoga: It implicitly contrasts conditional, desire-driven worship with pure, unconditional devotion to the Supreme Lord, which is the path to ultimate freedom.
  • The Gunas: The concept of prakṛti and niyatāḥ svayā directly links to the influence of the three gunas on human behavior and spiritual choices.

Practical Application

This verse offers profound insights that are highly relevant to modern life:

  1. Examine Your Motivations: In our highly consumerist and goal-oriented society, it’s easy to get swept away by desires for material possessions, career success, social status, or even specific spiritual experiences. This verse prompts us to pause and ask: What truly motivates my actions, including my spiritual or ethical practices? Am I seeking external validation, temporary comfort, or a deeper, more enduring connection with truth and purpose? Recognizing the “desires that steal wisdom” allows us to re-evaluate our priorities.

  2. Beyond Transactional Spirituality: Many people approach religion or spirituality with a “what’s in it for me?” mindset. We pray for good grades, a promotion, a healthy relationship, or relief from illness. While it’s natural to seek help, this verse encourages us to move beyond a purely transactional relationship with the divine. Instead of seeing God or the universe as a vending machine for our desires, we can cultivate a more profound, unconditional devotion – one that seeks spiritual growth, wisdom, and connection, irrespective of immediate material outcomes. This shift fosters resilience and inner peace.

  3. Understanding Human Diversity in Belief: This verse helps us understand why people choose different spiritual paths or deities. It’s often a reflection of their innate nature (prakṛti), their current desires, and their level of spiritual understanding. This fosters empathy and reduces judgment towards those whose beliefs or practices differ from our own. Instead of seeing other paths as “wrong,” we can view them as different stages or expressions of a universal human yearning, guided by individual conditioning and immediate needs. It reminds us that the journey is personal and often starts from seeking solutions to immediate problems.

Reflection

  • What specific desires in my life currently feel most compelling, and how might they be influencing my choices, including my spiritual outlook?
  • Do I approach my spiritual practices with a “shopping list” of requests, or with a deeper aspiration for connection and understanding?
  • How can I cultivate a more discerning intellect (jñāna) to see beyond immediate gratification and towards ultimate truth?

Key Concepts

  • Desire’s Influence (Kāmaiḥ Hṛtajñānāḥ): The power of material desires to obscure true wisdom and lead individuals away from the ultimate source of fulfillment.
  • Conditional Worship (Anya-devatāḥ): The practice of seeking specific boons from particular deities or forces, often driven by a transactional mindset rather than unconditional devotion.
  • Nature’s Compulsion (Prakṛtyā Niyatāḥ Svayā): How one’s inherent tendencies, shaped by the gunas and past karma, determine their desires, choices, and preferred modes of worship.
  • Discriminative Wisdom (Jñāna): The critical importance of an unclouded intellect to discern the temporary from the eternal, and the limited from the unlimited.
