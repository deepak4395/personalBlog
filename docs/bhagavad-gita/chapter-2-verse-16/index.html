The Eternal Truth: Distinguishing Real from Unreal

This profound verse establishes the fundamental distinction between the transient unreal and the eternal real, asserting that only truth endures.

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 16

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

नासतो विद्यते भावो नाभावो विद्यते सतः। उभयोरपि दृष्टोऽन्तस्त्वनयोस्तत्त्वदर्शिभिः॥

Transliteration (IAST)

nāsato vidyate bhāvo nābhāvo vidyate sataḥ | ubhayorapi dṛṣṭo'ntastvanayostattvadarśibhiḥ ||

Translation

The unreal has no existence; the real never ceases to be. The seers of truth have perceived the essential nature of both.

Explanation

Context

Verse 2.16 marks a pivotal moment in Krishna’s discourse to Arjuna. Having just outlined the imperishable nature of the soul (Atman) and the perishable nature of the body in previous verses, Krishna now lays down a fundamental metaphysical principle. He is systematically dismantling Arjuna’s grief and delusion, which stem from identifying with the transient world and its relationships. This verse provides the philosophical bedrock for understanding why one should not grieve over what is inherently impermanent, nor fear for what is eternally real. It introduces the core tenets of Sankhya philosophy, which posits a clear distinction between the unchanging ‘Purusha’ (consciousness/spirit) and the ever-changing ‘Prakriti’ (matter/nature).

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • नासतः (nāsataḥ): न (na - not) + असतः (asataḥ - of the non-existent, the unreal). Thus, “of that which is not real.”
  • विद्यते (vidyate): exists, is found, has being.
  • भावो (bhāvo): state of being, existence, reality.
  • नाभावो (nābhāvo): न (na - not) + अभावो (abhāvo - non-existence, absence of being). “Non-existence.”
  • विद्यते (vidyate): exists, is found.
  • सतः (sataḥ): of the existent, the real, the eternal truth.
  • उभयोः (ubhayoḥ): of both (referring to sat and asat).
  • अपि (api): also, even.
  • दृष्टो (dṛṣṭo): seen, perceived, understood.
  • अन्तः (antaḥ): end, conclusion, ultimate truth, essential nature.
  • त्वनयोः (tvanayoḥ): तु (tu - but, and) + अनयोः (anayoḥ - of these two). “And of these two.”
  • तत्त्वदर्शिभिः (tattvadarśibhiḥ): by the seers of truth/reality. Tattva means truth or reality, and darśibhiḥ means by those who see or perceive.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna here presents a foundational principle of Vedic philosophy: the clear distinction between Sat (the Real) and Asat (the Unreal).

Sat (The Real): This refers to the eternal, immutable, and ultimate reality. In the context of the Gita, Sat primarily denotes the Atman (the individual soul) and, by extension, Brahman (the Supreme Reality or Universal Consciousness). Sat is that which truly exists, which is beyond the cycles of birth and death, change, and decay. It is the permanent, unchanging substratum of all existence. It has no beginning and no end; it simply is.

Asat (The Unreal): This refers to that which is transient, impermanent, and subject to change. This includes the physical body, the material world, sensory experiences, emotions, thoughts, and all phenomena that have a beginning and an end. While Asat appears to exist, its existence is conditional and temporary, much like a dream or an illusion. It is not ultimately real in the same way Sat is.

Krishna’s teaching is that the Asat can never truly be in an ultimate sense (it has no enduring existence), and the Sat can never cease to be (it is eternal). This profound insight is not mere intellectual speculation but a direct perception for those who are tattvadarśibhiḥ – the seers of truth. These are individuals who have transcended superficial understanding and, through spiritual discipline and wisdom, have directly experienced the essential nature (antaḥ) of both the real and the unreal. They discern the eternal amidst the ephemeral, the unchanging core amidst the flux of existence.

This teaching directly counters Arjuna’s lament. If Arjuna understands that the bodies he fears to destroy are Asat (unreal in their ultimate nature, destined for change and decay) and the souls within them are Sat (eternal and indestructible), then his grief and fear become unfounded. This verse is key to developing Vairagya (detachment) and understanding the true nature of Self, which is essential for embarking on the path of Yoga.

Practical Application

  1. Cultivating Detachment from Outcomes: In daily life, we often invest heavily in the outcomes of our actions – career success, financial gain, social approval. Recognizing these as Asat (transient) can help us perform our duties with dedication but without crippling anxiety about results. We learn to act for the sake of action (karma yoga) rather than being enslaved by expectations, understanding that the true reward lies in righteous effort, not fleeting external validation.
  2. Managing Loss and Change: Life is full of changes – loss of loved ones, health challenges, shifting circumstances. By contemplating Sat and Asat, we can develop resilience. While acknowledging the pain of losing Asat (e.g., a loved one’s physical presence), we can also reflect on the eternal Sat (the soul’s continuity, the unchanging essence of love itself), which helps us navigate grief with greater equanimity and find deeper meaning.
  3. Prioritizing the Eternal: In a consumer-driven world, we are constantly bombarded with desires for new possessions and experiences. This verse encourages us to periodically evaluate what truly holds lasting value. Dedicating time to spiritual growth, self-reflection, meaningful relationships, and service to others – aspects that connect us to the Sat – can bring more profound and enduring satisfaction than chasing fleeting material pleasures.

Reflection

  • What aspects of my life do I mistakenly cling to as permanent, causing me distress when they inevitably change?
  • How can I consciously shift my focus from the transient (Asat) to the eternal (Sat) in my daily thoughts and actions?
  • In what ways can I cultivate the insight of a ‘seer of truth’ (tattvadarśibhiḥ) in my own life, discerning the essential nature of reality?

Key Concepts

  1. Sat (The Real/Eternal): The ultimate, unchanging, and permanent reality, synonymous with the Atman or Brahman.
  2. Asat (The Unreal/Transient): The temporary, ever-changing, and impermanent phenomena of the material world, including the body and sensory experiences.
  3. Impermanence vs. Permanence: The fundamental distinction between that which is subject to birth, growth, decay, and death, and that which is beyond these cycles.
  4. Tattvadarśibhiḥ (Seers of Truth): Individuals who, through spiritual wisdom and direct experience, have perceived the essential nature of both the real and the unreal.
