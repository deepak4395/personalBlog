The Eternal Witness: Krishna's Omniscience and Our Forgetfulness

Krishna reveals His eternal nature, stating He remembers countless past births, unlike Arjuna, emphasizing His divine awareness and timeless presence.

Chapter 4: Jnana Karma Sanyasa Yoga

Verse 5

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

श्रीभगवानुवाच बहूनि मे व्यतीतानि जन्मानि तव चार्जुन | तान्यहं वेद सर्वाणि न त्वं वेत्थ परंतप || ५ ||

Transliteration (IAST)

śrībhagavānuvāca bahūni me vyatītāni janmāni tava cārjuna | tānyahaṁ veda sarvāṇi na tvaṁ vettha paraṁtapa || 5 ||

Translation

The Blessed Lord said: Many births of Mine have passed, and of yours also, O Arjuna. I know all of them, but you do not, O scorcher of foes.

Explanation

Context

This profound verse marks a pivotal moment in the dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna. In the preceding verses (4.1-4.4), Krishna has declared that He taught this timeless yoga to Vivasvan (the sun-god) in ancient times, a statement that naturally perplexed Arjuna. Arjuna, viewing Krishna as his contemporary charioteer and friend, questioned how Krishna could have existed at such an ancient time. Verse 4.5 is Krishna’s direct response to this query, initiating a deeper revelation of His divine nature. It sets the stage for understanding the true identity of the Avatar, distinguishing the Supreme Being’s ‘births’ from the ordinary transmigration of a human soul. This explanation is crucial for Arjuna (and us) to grasp that Krishna is not merely a human hero but the very source of existence, operating beyond the constraints of time and memory that bind mortals.

Word-by-Word Meaning

Let’s break down the key terms in this verse to appreciate its depth:

  • बहूनि (bahūni): ‘Many’ – Emphasizing the countless nature of these occurrences.
  • मे (me): ‘Of Mine’ – Referring to Krishna’s own experiences.
  • व्यतीतानि (vyatītāni): ‘Have passed’ or ‘have gone by’ – Indicating events in the past.
  • जन्मानि (janmāni): ‘Births’ or ‘incarnations’ – The appearances in various forms.
  • तव (tava): ‘Of yours’ – Referring to Arjuna’s past lives.
  • च (ca): ‘And’ – Connecting Krishna’s and Arjuna’s experiences.
  • अर्जुन (arjuna): ‘O Arjuna’ – The direct address to the disciple.
  • तानि (tāni): ‘Those’ – Referring back to the many births.
  • अहं (ahaṁ): ‘I’ – Krishna, the speaker.
  • वेद (veda): ‘Know’ or ‘remember’ – Signifying complete awareness and recollection.
  • सर्वाणि (sarvāṇi): ‘All’ – Underscoring the comprehensive nature of Krishna’s knowledge.
  • न (na): ‘Not’ – A negation.
  • त्वं (tvaṁ): ‘You’ – Referring to Arjuna.
  • वेत्थ (vettha): ‘Know’ or ‘remember’ – The same verb as ‘veda’, but in the second person.
  • परंतप (paraṁtapa): ‘O scorcher of foes’ – An epithet for Arjuna, often used to inspire him to overcome internal and external obstacles, including ignorance. It implies that just as he can conquer enemies, he can conquer spiritual ignorance.

Deeper Understanding

This verse is a foundational statement about the nature of the Divine in the Bhagavad Gita. Krishna is not just stating a fact; He is revealing a profound truth about His identity and the distinction between the Supreme Self and the individual soul.

  • What is Krishna teaching Arjuna? Krishna is establishing His identity as the Supreme Being (Parabrahman), who is unborn (aja) and imperishable (avyaya), yet capable of appearing in the world through His own divine power (maya). Unlike ordinary beings who are bound by the laws of karma and are compelled to take birth, Krishna’s ‘births’ are voluntary descents (avatars) for specific purposes, primarily the re-establishment of Dharma. He is teaching Arjuna that His existence transcends linear time and human memory.

  • Key concepts presented:

    • Divine Omniscience: Krishna’s declaration, ‘I know all of them,’ highlights His absolute, all-encompassing knowledge. He is the eternal witness (Sākṣī), aware of all past, present, and future events, including the countless cycles of creation and dissolution. This perfect memory is a hallmark of His divinity.
    • Human Limitation and Forgetfulness: In contrast, Arjuna, representing the individual soul (jiva) entangled in the material world, ‘does not know’ his past births. This forgetfulness is a characteristic of conditioned consciousness, veiled by Maya. The jiva takes on a new body in each life, and the memories of previous lives are generally erased, allowing for a fresh start and preventing the overwhelming burden of countless past experiences.
    • Nature of the Avatar: This verse subtly introduces the concept of the Avatar. Krishna’s ‘births’ are not karmic reactions but deliberate manifestations. He retains His divine consciousness and knowledge throughout these appearances, unlike the jiva, which is reborn into ignorance.
    • Transmigration of the Soul: Implicitly, the verse confirms the concept of reincarnation for both Krishna and Arjuna. While Krishna’s ‘births’ are divine descents, Arjuna’s ‘births’ are part of the transmigration cycle, driven by karma. Both have existed for countless ages, but only one remembers the journey.

  • How does this relate to Dharma, Karma, Yoga, or other Gita themes?

    • Dharma: Krishna’s understanding of all past events allows Him to discern the true state of Dharma and intervene when it declines, as He explains in the very next verse (4.7).
    • Karma: The verse differentiates between karmic rebirth (Arjuna) and divine descent (Krishna). Krishna is beyond the reactions of karma, while Arjuna’s experiences are shaped by his past actions, the memories of which are hidden from him.
    • Yoga: Understanding Krishna’s divine nature and omniscience is a form of Jnana Yoga (Yoga of Knowledge). It cultivates devotion (Bhakti Yoga) by recognizing the Supreme Lord’s greatness and inspires selfless action (Karma Yoga) by aligning one’s will with the divine purpose.

Practical Application

This ancient wisdom holds profound relevance for the modern reader:

  1. Cultivating Humility and Trust: In our fast-paced, knowledge-driven world, we often believe we can know everything. This verse reminds us of our inherent limitations. Acknowledging that there are realms of existence and knowledge beyond our immediate grasp can foster humility. It encourages us to trust in a higher wisdom, whether divine, universal, or embodied in enlightened teachers, especially when faced with life’s inexplicable events or profound questions. It encourages surrender to a plan greater than our own.
  2. Mindfulness and Present Moment Awareness: While we don’t remember past lives, we are constantly creating our future through our present actions. The forgetfulness of past lives can be seen as a mercy, allowing us to focus on the ‘now’ without being burdened by an infinite past. This inspires us to act consciously, ethically, and with purpose in the present, understanding that every choice shapes our future, even if its ultimate unfolding is beyond our current memory.
  3. Seeking Deeper Knowledge: Arjuna’s question, though born of limited understanding, was a quest for truth. This verse encourages us to move beyond superficial perceptions and seek deeper spiritual knowledge. Even if we cannot recall past lives, we can strive to understand the eternal nature of the soul, the laws of karma, and the divine plan through scripture, contemplation, and spiritual practice. This pursuit of wisdom helps us transcend the limitations of our immediate sensory and intellectual experience.

  • How can the recognition of human limitations inspire you to seek spiritual knowledge and cultivate a more expansive worldview?
