The Diverse Paths of Sacrifice: From Deities to Brahman

Krishna reveals two fundamental approaches to spiritual sacrifice: offerings to the gods versus offering the very act of sacrifice into Brahman.

July 21, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Jnana Karma Sanyasa Yoga
4.25

Chapter 4: Jnana Karma Sanyasa Yoga

Verse 25

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

दैवं एवापरे यज्ञं योगिनः पर्युपासते ब्रह्माग्नावपरे यज्ञं यज्ञेनैवोपजुह्वति॥

Transliteration (IAST)

daivaṁ evāpare yajñaṁ yoginaḥ paryupāsate brahmāgnāvapare yajñaṁ yajñenaivopajuhvati.

Translation

Some yogis meticulously worship the gods through external sacrifices, while others offer the sacrifice itself into the fire of Brahman.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 4 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled ‘Jnana Karma Sanyasa Yoga’ or ‘The Yoga of Knowledge, Action, and Renunciation,’ delves deeply into the nature of karma, knowledge, and various forms of sacrifice (yajna). Krishna has been explaining to Arjuna how action, when performed with wisdom and detachment, can lead to liberation rather than bondage. He has already introduced the concept that true action is not merely physical activity but the inner attitude with which it is performed. Verse 24 laid the philosophical groundwork by declaring that Brahman is the offering, the offered, the fire, and the one who offers – implying that the entire act of sacrifice is Brahman. Building upon this, Verse 25 begins to illustrate the practical manifestations of this principle by describing different categories of ‘yogis’ and their distinct approaches to sacrifice, highlighting the diversity of spiritual paths that all ultimately aim for the same liberation.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • दैवं (daivaṁ): Pertaining to the gods or divine beings (devas).
  • एवापरे (evāpare): eva (indeed, certainly) + apare (others). So, ‘others, indeed’.
  • यज्ञं (yajñaṁ): Sacrifice, ritual offering. In the Gita, this term expands beyond mere ritual to encompass any dedicated action.
  • योगिनः (yoginaḥ): Yogis; those who practice yoga, spiritual aspirants, or those striving for union with the Divine.
  • पर्युपासते (paryupāsate): They worship, adore, attend upon, or perform. It implies a dedicated, devotional practice.
  • ब्रह्माग्नौ (brahmāgnau): Brahma (the ultimate reality, the Absolute) + agnau (in the fire). Thus, ‘in the fire of Brahman’. This signifies a profound, internal, and knowledge-based offering.
  • यज्ञेनैव (yajñenaiva): yajñena (by the sacrifice) + eva (indeed). Meaning ‘by the sacrifice itself’. This points to the act of sacrifice becoming the offering.
  • उपजुह्वति (upajuhvati): They offer, pour into (the fire). This is the act of performing the offering.

Combining these, the verse describes two distinct types of yogis: those who perform traditional sacrifices to the gods, and others who offer the very act of sacrifice into the ultimate reality, Brahman.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna, as the divine teacher, is presenting a spectrum of spiritual practices, all valid and leading towards the ultimate goal, but differing in their conceptual depth. He outlines two primary forms of ‘yajna’ here:

  1. Daiva Yajna (Sacrifice to the Gods): This refers to the traditional Vedic rituals and devotional practices where offerings are made to specific deities (devas) like Indra, Agni, Vayu, etc. These sacrifices are often performed with a specific intention, such as gaining material prosperity, heavenly abode, protection, or purification. The yogis engaging in this path are sincere in their devotion and adhere to prescribed rituals. This path emphasizes external worship and a dualistic understanding where the worshipper, the worshipped deity, and the offering are distinct entities. It’s a valid starting point for many, cultivating discipline, humility, and a sense of connection to the divine.

  2. Brahma Yajna (Sacrifice into the Fire of Brahman): This represents a more advanced, philosophical, and non-dualistic approach. Here, the yogi does not offer to specific deities but offers the very act of sacrifice itself – its components, its process, and its outcome – into the fire of Brahman. This implies a profound realization, as introduced in verse 24, that the entire universe, including the act of sacrifice, is an manifestation of Brahman. The ‘fire of Brahman’ is not a literal fire but the purifying fire of ultimate knowledge, where all distinctions dissolve. In this understanding, the offerer, the offering, and the recipient are all recognized as Brahman. This is the path of Jnana Yoga, where intellectual understanding and direct experience of the Absolute supersede external ritual. It’s a journey from multiplicity to unity, from form to the formless.

Krishna teaches Arjuna that both paths are legitimate forms of ‘yoga’ or spiritual endeavor. The Gita is incredibly inclusive, acknowledging that individuals are at different stages of spiritual evolution and have varying temperaments. While the Daiva Yajna focuses on devotion and ritual, the Brahma Yajna emphasizes knowledge and the realization of non-duality. Both, when performed with sincerity and the right understanding (even if partial), contribute to spiritual progress and the purification of the mind.

Practical Application

  1. Elevating Daily Actions: We can apply the principle of ‘Brahma Yajna’ to our daily lives. Instead of seeing our work, duties, or even simple acts of kindness as mundane tasks, we can offer them to a higher principle. Whether it’s doing our job with dedication, cooking a meal with love, or helping a neighbor, we can mentally offer the ‘sacrifice’ (our effort, time, and intention) into the ‘fire’ of our highest ideal, be it God, truth, or the well-being of all. This transforms ordinary actions into spiritual practices.
  2. Understanding Diverse Spiritual Paths: In our multi-faceted world, it’s easy to judge or dismiss spiritual practices different from our own. This verse encourages a broader perspective. Whether someone is praying in a temple, meditating in silence, performing social service, or engaging in devotional singing, all these can be legitimate forms of ‘yajna.’ Recognizing this helps foster tolerance, respect, and a deeper understanding of the universal human quest for meaning and connection.
  3. Moving Beyond Externalities: While rituals and external forms of worship can be supportive, the verse nudges us towards an inner understanding. We can reflect on the ‘why’ behind our actions and beliefs. Are we performing a ritual out of habit, or with a conscious awareness of its deeper significance? Are we seeking external rewards, or the internal realization of unity? This encourages us to gradually internalize our spiritual practice, moving from outward forms to inner transformation.

Reflection

  • How do my daily actions reflect a sense of offering or sacrifice?
  • Am I open to understanding and respecting spiritual paths different from my own?
  • How can I cultivate a deeper, more internal ‘fire of Brahman’ in my spiritual practice?

Key Concepts

  1. Daiva Yajna (Sacrifice to Deities): Traditional, devotional offerings to specific gods.
  2. Brahma Yajna (Sacrifice into Brahman): A profound, knowledge-based offering where the entire act of sacrifice is seen as Brahman itself.
  3. Diversity of Spiritual Paths: Acknowledgment of various valid approaches to spiritual realization.
  4. Internal vs. External Sacrifice: The shift from outward ritual to an inner realization of the divine in all aspects of life.
