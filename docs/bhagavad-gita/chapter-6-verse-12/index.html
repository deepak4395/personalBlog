The Foundation of Meditation: One-Pointed Mind for Self-Purification

This verse instructs the yogi to sit on a designated seat, focus the mind, control senses, and practice yoga for self-purification.

Chapter 6: Atma Samyama Yoga

Verse 12

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

तत्रैकाग्रं मनः कृत्वा यतचित्तेन्द्रियक्रियः। उपविश्यासने युञ्ज्याद्योगमात्मविशुद्धये।।

Transliteration (IAST)

tatraikāgraṁ manaḥ kṛtvā yatacittendriyakriyaḥ। upaviśyāsane yuñjyādyogamātmaviśuddhaye।।

Translation

Sitting there on that seat, making the mind one-pointed, and controlling the actions of the mind and senses, one should practice Yoga for the purification of the self.

Explanation

Context

In Chapter 6, titled ‘Atma Samyama Yoga’ (The Yoga of Self-Control), Lord Krishna is systematically guiding Arjuna through the practical steps of meditation and self-mastery. Verses 10 and 11 describe the ideal environment and the proper seat for a yogi to commence meditation. This verse, 6.12, now moves into the crucial internal and external preparations required while seated, emphasizing the mental discipline necessary for effective yogic practice. It lays down the immediate actions and the ultimate purpose of sitting in meditation, bridging the physical setup with the internal spiritual endeavor.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • तत्रैकाग्रं (tatraikāgraṁ): tatra (there, on that seat) + ekāgraṁ (one-pointed). This signifies the absolute necessity of focusing the mind on a single point, preventing its natural tendency to wander.
  • मनः कृत्वा (manaḥ kṛtvā): ‘having made the mind’. This implies a deliberate, conscious effort to bring the mind to a state of focus.
  • यतचित्तेन्द्रियक्रियः (yatacittendriyakriyaḥ): yata (controlled, restrained) + citta (mind-stuff, consciousness) + indriya (senses) + kriyaḥ (actions). This is a composite term meaning ‘one who has controlled the actions of the mind and senses.’ It highlights the dual control required: both the internal mental processes and the external sensory perceptions.
  • उपविश्यासने (upaviśyāsane): upaviśya (sitting) + āsane (on the seat). Reiteration of the posture and the designated place, reinforcing the stability required.
  • युञ्ज्यात् (yuñjyāt): ‘should practice’ or ‘should engage in Yoga’. It denotes the active, purposeful engagement in the practice.
  • योगं (yogam): ‘Yoga’, referring here specifically to the practice of meditation (Dhyana Yoga) aimed at union with the Divine.
  • आत्मविशुद्धये (ātmaviśuddhaye): ātma (self) + viśuddhaye (for purification). This is the profound ultimate goal of the practice: the purification of the inner self, leading to clarity, self-realization, and freedom from impurities and delusions.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s instruction here is foundational to any serious spiritual practice involving meditation. He is teaching Arjuna that merely finding a quiet spot and a comfortable seat is insufficient. The real work begins with mental discipline. The concept of ekāgratā (one-pointedness) is paramount. The human mind is inherently restless, jumping from thought to thought. To achieve ekāgratā is to tame this restlessness, allowing for deeper introspection and concentration.

The control over citta (mind-stuff) and indriya (senses) is a direct precursor to higher states of meditation. This aligns with Patanjali’s Yoga Sutras, where Pratyahara (withdrawal of senses) and Dharana (concentration) are critical limbs of Ashtanga Yoga. Without this control, the mind remains fragmented, easily distracted by external stimuli or internal chatter, making true meditation impossible.

The ultimate purpose, ātmaviśuddhaye (for the purification of the self), elevates the practice beyond mere mental exercise or stress relief. It’s a spiritual journey to cleanse the self of ego, desires, attachments, and ignorance that obscure its true divine nature. This purification leads to a state of clarity where one can perceive the Atman, the true Self, free from the coverings of the material world. It connects to the overarching Gita themes of transcending the gunas, attaining liberation (moksha), and realizing the oneness of the individual soul with the Supreme.

Practical Application

  1. Designate a Sacred Space and Time: Just as the verse emphasizes a specific seat, create a dedicated, quiet space in your home for meditation. Consistency in time and place helps train the mind to enter a meditative state more easily. Even a small corner can become your ‘āsana’.
  2. Practice Mindful Focus (Ekāgratā): Start by observing your breath for a few minutes daily. When the mind wanders, gently bring it back to the breath without judgment. This continuous effort of redirecting the mind builds the ‘one-pointedness’ muscle, which can then be applied to tasks in daily life, improving concentration and efficiency.
  3. Conscious Sensory Regulation: Before meditation, consciously minimize distractions. Turn off notifications, dim lights, and seek a quiet environment. During meditation, when sounds or sensations arise, acknowledge them without engaging, gently returning your focus to your chosen anchor (mantra, breath). This practice extends to daily life by mindfully choosing what sensory input you consume (e.g., limiting screen time, choosing uplifting content).

Reflection

  • How does the concept of ‘one-pointedness’ resonate with your experience of daily focus and distraction?
  • What ‘impurities’ (mental patterns, attachments, desires) do you feel obscure your true self, and how might meditation help in their purification?
  • How can you integrate conscious control of your senses and mind into your daily routine, beyond formal meditation?

Key Concepts

  • Ekāgratā (One-pointedness of Mind): The state of having the mind focused on a single object or thought, essential for deep meditation.
  • Indriya-Citta Niyantrana (Control of Senses and Mind): The disciplined restraint of both external sensory perceptions and internal mental processes.
  • Ātmaviśuddhi (Self-Purification): The ultimate goal of yogic practice, leading to the cleansing of the inner self from ignorance, desires, and attachments.
  • Dhyana Yoga (Meditation Practice): The practical application of sitting in meditation with a controlled and focused mind for spiritual realization.
