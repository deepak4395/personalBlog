Unveiling the Root of Delusion: Bhagavad Gita Chapter 7, Verse 27

This verse reveals how all beings are born into delusion, ensnared by dualities arising from fundamental desires and aversions.

November 18, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Jnana Vijnana Yoga
7.27

Chapter 7: Jnana Vijnana Yoga

Verse 27

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

इच्छाद्वेषसमुत्थेन द्वन्द्वमोहेन भारत। सर्वभूतानि संमोहं सर्गे यान्ति परंतप॥

Transliteration (IAST)

icchādveṣasamutthena dvandvamohena bhārata। sarvabhūtāni saṃmohaṃ sarge yānti paraṃtapa॥

Translation

O Bharata, O scorcher of foes, all beings in this creation are born into delusion, overcome by the delusion of dualities arising from desire and aversion.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 7 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled ‘Jnana Vijnana Yoga’ (The Yoga of Knowledge and Realization), is where Lord Krishna begins to reveal the deeper truths of His divine nature, explaining how He is the ultimate reality underlying all existence. He describes Himself as the source of all, yet transcends all. He explains that attaining true knowledge of Him is difficult because most people are deluded by His illusory energy (Maya). In the verses leading up to 27, Krishna has just stated that ignorant people misunderstand His unmanifest nature, perceiving Him as an ordinary being, and thus fail to recognize His supreme, imperishable, and transcendental form. He knows all beings, but no one truly knows Him. Verse 27 then provides a profound explanation for this universal ignorance, pinpointing the very root cause of why beings are unable to perceive the ultimate truth.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • इच्छा (icchā): Desire, wish, longing. This refers to the fundamental drive to acquire or experience what one perceives as pleasant or beneficial.
  • द्वेष (dveṣa): Aversion, hatred, dislike. This is the natural reaction to avoid or repel what one perceives as unpleasant or harmful.
  • समुत्थेन (samutthena): Arisen from, born of, originating from. This indicates that the subsequent phenomenon is a direct consequence of desire and aversion.
  • द्वन्द्व (dvandva): Dualities, pairs of opposites. This encompasses all the contrasting experiences of life: pleasure and pain, gain and loss, honor and dishonor, heat and cold, success and failure, love and hate.
  • मोहेन (mohena): By delusion, by illusion, by infatuation. This signifies a state of mental confusion, a distorted perception of reality that obscures the truth.
  • भारत (bhārata): O descendant of Bharata. A respectful address to Arjuna, signifying his noble lineage.
  • सर्वभूतानि (sarvabhūtāni): All beings, all creatures. This emphasizes the universal nature of this condition, affecting every living entity.
  • संमोहं (saṃmohaṃ): Into complete delusion, utter bewilderment. The prefix ‘sam’ intensifies the ‘moha,’ indicating a deep and pervasive state of illusion.
  • सर्गे (sarge): At creation, at birth, in the process of manifestation. This implies that this delusion is inherent to the very act of being born into the material world.
  • यान्ति (yānti): Go, come, fall into, attain. They succumb to or enter this state of delusion.
  • परंतप (paraṃtapa): O scorcher of foes. Another epithet for Arjuna, acknowledging his prowess and capability, implicitly suggesting he has the strength to overcome this internal foe as well.

Deeper Understanding

In this pivotal verse, Krishna reveals the fundamental impediment to spiritual realization: the ‘delusion of dualities’ (dvandva-moha), which originates from ‘desire and aversion’ (iccha-dvesha). He asserts that all beings, from the moment of their birth into this creation, are enveloped in this profound delusion. This is a crucial teaching because it explains why it is so challenging for individuals to perceive the ultimate reality, Krishna’s true nature, and to live a life free from suffering.

The human experience is largely defined by our reactions to the world around us. We are constantly drawn towards what we desire (pleasure, comfort, success) and repelled by what we avert (pain, discomfort, failure). These two fundamental forces – desire and aversion – are the engines that drive our engagement with the material world. However, the Gita explains that these very forces give rise to the ‘dvandvas’ or dualities. We label experiences as ‘good’ or ‘bad,’ ‘likeable’ or ‘dislikeable,’ creating a mental framework that categorizes and judges everything. This constant labeling and reacting traps the mind in a cycle of attachment and aversion, joy and sorrow, hope and fear.

This ‘dvandva-moha’ is not just a superficial misunderstanding; it’s a deep-seated delusion that prevents us from seeing the underlying unity and truth of existence. Instead, we perceive a fragmented world, driven by our personal preferences and biases. This prevents us from recognizing the divine presence in all things and understanding our true spiritual identity beyond the temporary pairs of opposites. It directly relates to the concept of Maya – the divine illusory power that makes the relative world appear absolutely real and obscures the absolute truth. The verse highlights that this delusion is not an acquired trait but is inherent to the condition of being born into the material realm (sarge yānti). This implies that overcoming it requires conscious and sustained spiritual effort.

Practical Application

  1. Cultivate Self-Awareness: Begin by observing your own desires and aversions in daily life. Notice how strongly you react to praise or criticism, comfort or discomfort, gain or loss. Simply observing these patterns without judgment is the first step towards loosening their grip. For instance, when you receive a compliment (desire fulfilled) or face a minor setback (aversion triggered), pause and acknowledge the emotional reaction without letting it overwhelm you.
  2. Practice Equanimity: Consciously work on developing an even-minded approach to the dualities of life. Understand that pleasure and pain, success and failure, are temporary and cyclical. When faced with a challenging situation, instead of immediately reacting with aversion, try to see it as an opportunity for growth or a natural part of life’s ebb and flow. Similarly, do not become overly attached to moments of success or joy, understanding their impermanent nature.
  3. Mindful Detachment: Engage with the world and perform your duties (karma) without being solely attached to the outcomes. This doesn’t mean becoming indifferent, but rather understanding that while effort is within your control, results are not always. For example, in a professional project, give your best effort, but be prepared to accept various outcomes without excessive elation or despair. This practice helps to reduce the ‘moha’ or delusion that ties your happiness solely to external circumstances.

Reflection

  • How do my immediate desires and aversions shape my daily decisions and emotional states?
  • Can I identify specific ‘dvandvas’ (dualities) that have a strong hold on my mind? How do I react to them?
  • What steps can I take today to consciously observe my reactions to pleasure and pain, success and failure, and begin to cultivate a more balanced perspective?

Key Concepts

  • Dvandva Moha (Delusion of Dualities): The central idea that human perception is clouded by attachments to pairs of opposites (pleasure/pain, good/bad), preventing a clear view of reality.
  • Iccha-Dvesha (Desire and Aversion): Identified as the fundamental psychological forces that give rise to the delusion of dualities, driving all beings’ engagement with the world.
  • Universal Delusion: The teaching that this state of delusion affects ‘all beings from birth’ (sarvabhūtāni sarge yānti), highlighting its pervasive and inherent nature in the material existence.
  • Spiritual Ignorance: The ultimate consequence of dvandva-moha, which prevents individuals from recognizing the Supreme Truth and their own true, divine nature.
