The Equanimous Sage: Steadfast in Brahman

This verse describes the characteristics of a liberated soul who remains undisturbed by life's dualities, possessing steady intellect and abiding in Brahman.

Chapter 5: Karma Sanyasa Yoga

Verse 20

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

न प्रहृष्येत्प्रियं प्राप्य नोद्विजेत्प्राप्य चाप्रियम्। स्थिरबुद्धिरसंमूढो ब्रह्मविद् ब्रह्मणि स्थितः॥

Transliteration (IAST)

na prahṛṣyetpriyaṁ prāpya nodvijetprāpya cāpriyam | sthirabuddhirasaṁmūḍho brahmavid brahmaṇi sthitaḥ ||

Translation

One who neither rejoices upon obtaining what is pleasant nor laments upon meeting what is unpleasant, who is steady in intellect, unbewildered, and knows the Absolute Truth, is established in Brahman.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 5 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled ‘Karma Sanyasa Yoga’, explores the path of renunciation through action. Arjuna is still confused about whether action (karma yoga) or renunciation of action (sannyasa) is superior. Krishna, through this chapter, clarifies that true renunciation is not about giving up external actions, but about an internal detachment from their fruits and a clear understanding of one’s true nature. This verse, following discussions on the impartial vision of a wise person (verse 18) and the understanding that the self is not the doer (verse 14), delves deeper into the characteristics of such a liberated individual. It describes the mental state and realization of one who has truly understood the essence of Brahman, setting the stage for further elaboration on meditative practices and the nature of ultimate peace.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • न प्रहृष्येत् (na prahṛṣyet): not rejoice, does not become elated.
  • प्रियं (priyaṁ): pleasant, agreeable, dear.
  • प्राप्य (prāpya): having obtained, having reached.
  • नोद्विजेत् (nodvijet): nor be agitated, nor lament, nor be distressed (from ‘na’ + ‘udvijet’).
  • प्राप्य (prāpya): having obtained, having met.
  • च (ca): and.
  • अप्रियम् (apriyam): unpleasant, disagreeable, undesirable.
  • स्थिरबुद्धिः (sthirabuddhiḥ): steady in intellect, firm in understanding (from ‘sthira’ - steady, and ‘buddhi’ - intellect).
  • असंमूढः (asaṁmūḍhaḥ): unbewildered, undeluded, not confused (from ‘a’ - not, and ‘saṁmūḍha’ - bewildered, deluded).
  • ब्रह्मवित् (brahmavit): one who knows Brahman, the knower of the Absolute Truth (from ‘Brahman’ - the Absolute Reality, and ‘vit’ - knower).
  • ब्रह्मणि (brahmaṇi): in Brahman, in the Absolute Truth.
  • स्थितः (sthitaḥ): established, situated.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna, the divine teacher, is here describing the epitome of a liberated soul, one who has transcended the dualities of the material world. This verse beautifully encapsulates the state of samatvam (equanimity), a core teaching of the Gita. The individual described here is not merely suppressing reactions, but has genuinely risen above the sway of pleasure and pain. This detachment isn’t a forced indifference but a natural outcome of profound spiritual realization.

The key concepts presented are:

  1. Freedom from Dualities: The initial phrase, na prahṛṣyetpriyaṁ prāpya nodvijetprāpya cāpriyam, speaks to the ability to remain unperturbed by life’s ups and downs. Whether it’s a desired outcome (pleasant) or an undesired one (unpleasant), the mind remains calm and steady. This is a hallmark of spiritual maturity, indicating a shift from ego-centric reactions to a more expansive, self-aware state.
  2. Steady Intellect (Sthirabuddhi): This refers to an intellect that is firm, unwavering, and not easily swayed by external circumstances or internal emotions. Such a person possesses discernment and wisdom, understanding the transient nature of worldly experiences and the eternal nature of the Self. This echoes the concept of the sthitaprajña (steady-minded person) introduced in Chapter 2.
  3. Freedom from Delusion (Asaṁmūḍha): The term ‘asaṁmūḍha’ signifies being free from delusion or bewilderment. The greatest delusion is identifying oneself with the temporary body, mind, and ego, rather than the eternal Atman (Self). An ‘asaṁmūḍha’ person has overcome this fundamental error, realizing their true, unchanging essence.
  4. Knower of Brahman (Brahmavit): This is the ultimate realization. ‘Brahman’ is the supreme, absolute reality, the ground of all existence. A ‘brahmavit’ is one who has directly experienced this ultimate truth, understanding the non-duality of the individual soul (Atman) and the universal Spirit (Brahman). This knowledge is not intellectual understanding but direct, experiential realization.
  5. Established in Brahman (Brahmaṇi sthitaḥ): This is the culmination of the process. It’s not enough to merely know Brahman; one must be established in that knowledge, living and acting from that state of oneness. All actions, thoughts, and perceptions arise from this profound realization, making their life a living embodiment of spiritual truth. Such a person performs actions without attachment, seeing the divine presence in all beings and situations.

This verse connects directly to the themes of Karma Yoga (performing action without attachment to results) and Jnana Yoga (the path of knowledge). The ‘brahmavit’ embodies the perfect synthesis of both – they perform their duties in the world, but their inner state is one of profound peace, wisdom, and identification with the Absolute, beyond the temporary play of dualities.

Practical Application

This ancient wisdom offers profound guidance for navigating contemporary life:

  1. Cultivating Emotional Resilience: In a world filled with constant highs and lows (social media validation, career successes, personal setbacks), practice observing your reactions without immediate judgment. When something pleasant happens, appreciate it without clinging. When something unpleasant occurs, acknowledge the feeling but avoid getting swept away by despair. Ask yourself, “Is this truly affecting my core being, or just my temporary circumstances?” This practice builds inner strength and prevents external events from dictating your inner peace.
  2. Developing Detached Observation: In your daily interactions and work, strive to perform your duties with sincerity and skill, but consciously detach from the outcome. For example, if you’re working on a project, give your best effort, but don’t let the project’s success or failure define your self-worth. This reduces stress, enhances focus, and allows you to learn from all experiences without the burden of egoic attachment.
  3. Mindfulness of True Identity: Regularly engage in practices that remind you of your deeper self, beyond your roles, possessions, or achievements. This could be through meditation, self-inquiry, or simply taking moments of quiet reflection. Understanding that your true self is unchanging and unaffected by external fluctuations helps you remain unbewildered (asaṁmūḍha) and steady (sthirabuddhi) amidst life’s challenges, fostering a sense of inner freedom.

Reflection

  • How often do I allow external praise or criticism to dictate my mood and self-perception?
  • What specific situations in my life cause me to lose my sthirabuddhi (steady intellect)? How can I respond differently next time?
  • In what ways can I cultivate a deeper understanding that my true self is distinct from my experiences of pleasure and pain?

Key Concepts

  • Equanimity (Samatvam): Remaining undisturbed by pleasure and pain.
  • Brahman-consciousness (Brahmajnana): Realization and establishment in the Absolute Truth.
  • Steady Intellect (Sthirabuddhi): Unwavering wisdom and discernment.
  • Freedom from Delusion (Asammudha): Overcoming the illusion of identifying with the temporary self.
