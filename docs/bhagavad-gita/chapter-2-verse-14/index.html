Enduring Dualities: The Path to Equanimity (Bhagavad Gita 2.14)

Krishna teaches Arjuna to tolerate the impermanent dualities of pleasure and pain, heat and cold, which arise from sense contact, as they are fleeting.

March 19, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga
2.14

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 14

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

मात्रास्पर्शास्तु कौन्तेय शीतोष्णसुखदुःखदाः। आगमापायिनोऽनित्यास्तांस्तितिक्षस्व भारत॥

Transliteration (IAST)

mātrāsparśāstu kaunteya śītoṣṇasukhaduḥkhadāḥ। āgamāpāyino'nityāstāṃstitikṣasva bhārata॥

Translation

O son of Kunti, the contacts of the senses with their objects give rise to feelings of heat and cold, pleasure and pain. They are impermanent and appear and disappear. Therefore, O Bharata, tolerate them.

Explanation

Context

In the second chapter, “Sankhya Yoga,” Arjuna is overwhelmed by grief and delusion, refusing to fight his kinsmen. Lord Krishna begins to impart profound spiritual wisdom to dispel Arjuna’s sorrow. Prior to this verse, Krishna has established the eternal nature of the soul (Atman) and the temporary nature of the body (2.11-13). He explains that the soul neither kills nor is killed, and that death is merely the changing of bodies. Having laid this metaphysical foundation, Krishna now transitions to the practical implications of this truth for one living in the material world. He addresses the transient nature of sensory experiences, preparing Arjuna to face the challenges of life and duty with an undisturbed mind. This verse serves as a bridge, moving from the philosophical understanding of the Self to the practical discipline required to maintain equanimity amidst life’s inevitable dualities.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • मात्रास्पर्शास्तु (mātrāsparśāstu): “But the contacts of the senses.” Mātrā refers to the elements or sense objects (like form, sound, touch, taste, smell), and sparśāḥ means contacts or interactions. So, mātrāsparśāḥ signifies the interactions between our sense organs (eyes, ears, skin, tongue, nose) and their respective objects in the external world.
  • कौन्तेय (kaunteya): “O son of Kunti.” An affectionate address to Arjuna, reminding him of his noble lineage and implicitly, his capacity for wisdom.
  • शीतोष्ण (śītoṣṇa): “Cold and heat.” These represent opposite sensations, emblematic of all pairs of opposites in life.
  • सुखदुःखदाः (sukhaduḥkhadāḥ): “Givers of pleasure and pain.” These are the emotional responses that arise from the sensory contacts, often directly linked to our preferences for heat/cold, soft/hard, etc.
  • आगमापायिनोऽनित्याः (āgamāpāyino’nityāḥ): “Coming and going; impermanent.” Āgamāpāyinaḥ means ‘appearing and disappearing’ or ‘coming and going.’ Anityāḥ means ‘not eternal,’ ‘temporary,’ or ‘transient.’ This phrase emphasizes the fleeting nature of these experiences.
  • तान् (tān): “Them.” Referring to these dualities of heat/cold, pleasure/pain.
  • तितिक्षस्व (titikṣasva): “Tolerate them; endure them.” This is the crucial instruction. It means to bear them patiently without being disturbed or overwhelmed.
  • भारत (bhārata): “O descendant of Bharata.” Another address to Arjuna, emphasizing his valor and historical legacy, urging him to live up to his potential.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s teaching here is foundational to spiritual practice and mental resilience. He is teaching Arjuna that the experiences of pleasure and pain, comfort and discomfort, are not inherent to our true Self (Atman) but are products of the interaction between our senses and the external world. Just as seasons change, bringing heat and cold, these sensations and the feelings they evoke are transient. They arise, they stay for a while, and then they disappear. They are anityāḥ – impermanent.

The core instruction is titikṣasva – tolerate them. This isn’t about suppressing feelings or becoming numb. Instead, titikṣā (tolerance or endurance) implies developing an inner strength to observe these dualities without being swayed or disturbed by them. It means recognizing their temporary nature and refusing to let them dictate one’s inner peace or actions. By cultivating this tolerance, one can remain steady in both favorable and unfavorable conditions.

This teaching relates directly to various Gita themes:

  • Dharma: To perform one’s duty (dharma) effectively, especially in a battlefield-like situation, one cannot be paralyzed by fear of pain or desire for comfort. Titikṣā enables one to act decisively without being attached to the outcomes or the sensory experiences involved.
  • Yoga: The path of Yoga aims for equanimity (samatvam). This verse is a direct instruction towards achieving that state. By tolerating dualities, one moves towards a state of mental balance, undisturbed by external circumstances.
  • Karma Yoga: For an individual engaged in action (karma), understanding the impermanence of pleasure and pain helps in developing non-attachment to the fruits of action. One performs duty not for pleasure or to avoid pain, but because it is one’s dharma.
  • Non-attachment (Vairagya): The recognition that all sensory experiences are fleeting naturally fosters a sense of detachment from them. When one ceases to identify with these transient experiences, the grip of attachment and aversion loosens.

Krishna’s wisdom here is a practical guide to living in the material world without being consumed by its fluctuating nature. It’s a call to shift our identification from the ever-changing body and mind to the eternal, unchanging Self.

Practical Application

  1. Emotional Resilience in Daily Life: When facing common stressors like a difficult commute (heat/cold, discomfort), a challenging project at work (mental pain/stress), or even a minor injury, consciously remind yourself, “This too shall pass.” Instead of reacting with immediate frustration or despair, observe the sensation or emotion, acknowledge its presence, and then consciously choose not to let it define your entire state of being. This practice helps build emotional fortitude.
  2. Mindful Consumption: In an age of instant gratification, we often seek pleasure and avoid discomfort. When eating a meal, for instance, notice the taste without clinging to the pleasure or being repulsed by a less preferred flavor. When exercising, observe the discomfort of exertion without immediately wanting to stop. This mindful approach helps cultivate a balanced relationship with sensory experiences, preventing overindulgence or excessive aversion.
  3. Navigating Life’s Ups and Downs: Life inevitably presents both successes and failures, praise and criticism. When experiencing a triumph, enjoy the moment but remember its transient nature, avoiding excessive pride or attachment. Similarly, during setbacks, acknowledge the pain or disappointment, but remind yourself that it is not permanent and does not diminish your essential worth. This perspective enables one to remain stable and continue moving forward, regardless of external circumstances.

Reflection

  • What are the “heats and colds” (pleasure and pain, comfort and discomfort) that I currently experience in my life?
  • How do I typically react to these sensations and emotions? Do I tend to cling to the pleasurable and resist the painful?
  • Can I practice observing these experiences without immediate judgment or intense emotional reaction, simply acknowledging their transient nature?
  • How might cultivating titikṣā (tolerance) help me navigate a current challenge or decision?

Key Concepts

  • Duality (Dvanda): The inherent pairs of opposites (pleasure/pain, heat/cold, honor/dishonor) that characterize material existence.
  • Impermanence (Anitya): The fundamental truth that all material phenomena and sensory experiences are temporary and subject to change.
  • Tolerance (Titiksha): The spiritual discipline of enduring or patiently bearing the dualities of life without being disturbed or losing one’s inner peace.
  • Sense Contact (Matrasparsha): The interaction between our senses and their objects, which is the origin point for all sensory experiences and their associated feelings.
