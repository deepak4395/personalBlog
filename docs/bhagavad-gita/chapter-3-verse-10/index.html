The Divine Ordinance of Sacrifice: Sustenance and Prosperity Through Karma Yoga

Prajapati created humanity along with sacrifice, declaring it the means for prosperity and the fulfiller of all desires, establishing a cosmic cycle of giving.

May 25, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Yoga
3.10

Chapter 3: Karma Yoga

Verse 10

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

सहयज्ञाः प्रजाः सृष्ट्वा पुरोवाच प्रजापतिः। अनेन प्रसविष्यध्वमेष वोऽस्त्विष्टकामधुक्॥

Transliteration (IAST)

sahayajñāḥ prajāḥ sṛṣṭvā purovāca prajāpatiḥ | anena prasaviṣyadhvameṣa vo'stviṣṭakāmadhuk ||

Translation

In the beginning, Prajapati (the Creator) created mankind along with sacrifices and said, "By this (sacrifice) you shall prosper and multiply; let it be the fulfiller of all your desires."

Explanation

Context

Chapter 3 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled ‘Karma Yoga,’ delves into the profound philosophy of action. Arjuna, confused by Krishna’s seemingly contradictory advice – sometimes extolling knowledge (Jnana Yoga) and at other times action (Karma Yoga) – seeks clarity on which path is superior. Krishna systematically addresses this by explaining that action is an inherent and unavoidable aspect of existence. He emphasizes that it is not action itself, but the motive and attachment to its fruits that bind an individual. This particular verse, 3.10, is pivotal. It moves beyond the individual’s obligation to act and introduces the cosmic dimension of action, establishing yajña (sacrifice or selfless action) as the foundational principle by which the entire creation is sustained and prospers. It sets the stage for understanding that human effort is not merely for personal gain but is deeply intertwined with the welfare and harmony of the universe.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • सहयज्ञाः (sahayajñāḥ): ‘along with sacrifices.’ This compound word highlights that yajña was not an afterthought but an intrinsic part of creation, created simultaneously with beings.
  • प्रजाः (prajāḥ): ‘beings,’ ‘mankind.’ Refers to all sentient life created.
  • सृष्ट्वा (sṛṣṭvā): ‘having created.’ Denotes the act of creation by the supreme architect.
  • पुरोवाच (purovāca): ‘in ancient times, said.’ Points to a primordial, foundational instruction or divine decree.
  • प्रजापतिः (prajāpatiḥ): ‘Lord of creation,’ ‘the Creator.’ The divine entity responsible for bringing forth the universe and its inhabitants.
  • अनेन (anena): ‘by this (sacrifice).’ Refers to yajña as the prescribed means.
  • प्रसविष्यध्वम् (prasaviṣyadhvam): ‘you shall prosper,’ ‘multiply,’ ‘grow.’ Implies both material and spiritual growth, continuation, and flourishing.
  • एषः (eṣaḥ): ‘this.’ Again referring to yajña.
  • वः (vaḥ): ‘for you.’ Denotes the benefit intended for humanity.
  • अस्तु (astu): ‘let it be.’ A divine command or blessing.
  • इष्टकामधुक् (iṣṭakāmadhuk): ‘fulfiller of desired objects,’ ‘bestower of all desires.’ This term evokes the mythical Kamadhenu, the wish-fulfilling cow, suggesting that yajña is the ultimate source of all fulfillment.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna is teaching Arjuna that the universe is not a random collection of events but operates on a divine principle of mutual sustenance, often termed yajña. This isn’t limited to ritualistic fire sacrifices but encompasses any selfless action performed as an offering to the divine or for the welfare of the collective. Prajapati, the Creator, didn’t just create beings; He simultaneously provided them with a mechanism for their own sustained existence and prosperity through the principle of giving.

The core concept here is interdependence. No being exists in isolation. We are all part of a vast cosmic ecosystem, and our actions have ripple effects. The act of yajña symbolizes this give-and-take. When we contribute selflessly – be it through our work, our resources, our knowledge, or our compassion – we are participating in this divine cycle. This participation ensures not only our own well-being but also the well-being of the entire creation. To take without giving back, or to act purely for selfish gain, disrupts this cosmic balance and ultimately leads to suffering.

This verse lays a strong foundation for Karma Yoga. It elevates daily actions from mundane tasks to sacred offerings. When we perform our duties with a spirit of yajña, understanding that our efforts contribute to a larger whole, we transcend the ego-driven desire for results. This detachment from outcomes, while maintaining full engagement in action, is the essence of Karma Yoga. It aligns our individual dharma with the universal dharma, leading to both material prosperity and spiritual liberation.

Practical Application

  1. Work as an Offering: In our professional lives, instead of viewing work solely as a means to a paycheck or personal ambition, we can reframe it as a form of yajña. By performing our duties with integrity, dedication, and a focus on how our work benefits others or society, we participate in the cosmic cycle of giving. For example, a doctor treating patients, an engineer building safe structures, a teacher educating minds – all can be seen as acts of yajña if done selflessly.
  2. Environmental Stewardship: Recognizing our dependence on nature (air, water, earth) and the need to give back. Acts like recycling, conserving resources, supporting sustainable practices, or engaging in conservation efforts become modern forms of yajña. We are offering back to the planet that sustains us.
  3. Community Engagement and Service: Volunteering time, sharing resources, mentoring others, or simply being a supportive neighbor are direct applications of this verse. These actions contribute to the prosperity and harmony of our immediate communities, extending the principle of mutual support beyond our immediate family.

Reflection

  • How do my daily actions reflect a spirit of giving and contribution to the larger world?
  • Am I conscious of the resources I consume and how I give back?
  • Can I identify areas in my life where I can transform a self-serving action into a selfless offering?

Key Concepts

  • Yajña (Selfless Action/Sacrifice): The cosmic principle of giving and contributing for the welfare of all, extending beyond ritual to encompass all actions.
  • Divine Ordinance: The understanding that creation is governed by a purposeful, divinely ordained system of mutual sustenance.
  • Interdependence: The fundamental truth that all beings and elements of the universe are interconnected and rely on each other.
  • Prosperity through Contribution: The teaching that true and lasting prosperity (material and spiritual) arises from selfless giving and participation in the cosmic cycle, rather than mere accumulation.
