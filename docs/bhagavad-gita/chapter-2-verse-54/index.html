Arjuna's Quest: Defining the Person of Steady Wisdom (Sthitaprajña)

Arjuna inquires about the characteristics and conduct of a person with steady wisdom, seeking to understand the nature of a self-realized soul.

April 27, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga #Sthitaprajña #Spiritual Wisdom #Self-Realization
2.54

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 54

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

अर्जुन उवाच स्थितप्रज्ञस्य का भाषा समाधिस्थस्य केशव । स्थितधीः किं प्रभाषेत किमासीत व्रजेत किम् ॥ ५४ ॥

Transliteration (IAST)

arjuna uvāca sthitaprajñasya kā bhāṣā samādhisthasya keśava | sthitadhīḥ kiṁ prabhāṣeta kimāsīta vrajeta kim || 54 ||

Translation

Arjuna said: What are the characteristics of a steady-minded person, O Krishna, one absorbed in samadhi? How does one of steady intellect speak, how does he sit, and how does he walk?

Explanation

Context

This verse marks a pivotal moment in the Bhagavad Gita, shifting from abstract philosophical discourse to the practical application of spiritual wisdom. Lord Krishna has just concluded a profound exposition on the impermanence of the body, the eternality of the soul (Atman), the importance of performing one’s duty (dharma) without attachment to results (karma yoga), and the nature of true knowledge (Sankhya yoga). Arjuna, ever the pragmatist and a man of action, now seeks to understand how these deep truths manifest in a living, breathing individual. He is not merely interested in theoretical knowledge; he wants a tangible description of what a self-realized soul looks like and how such a person conducts themselves in the world. This inquiry, spanning from verse 54 to 72 of Chapter 2, lays the foundation for Krishna’s detailed description of the sthitaprajña (person of steady wisdom), an ideal state of being that is central to the Gita’s teachings on practical spirituality and liberation.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • अर्जुन उवाच (Arjuna uvāca): Arjuna said.
  • स्थितप्रज्ञस्य (sthitaprajñasya): Of the steady-minded person; one whose intellect (prajñā) is firm, stable, or established (sthita).
  • का (kā): What.
  • भाषा (bhāṣā): Description, characteristic, mark, language. Here, it refers to the outward signs or definition by which such a person can be recognized.
  • समाधिस्थस्य (samādhisthasya): Of one established in samadhi; one whose mind is absorbed in deeper consciousness or spiritual equilibrium, not necessarily just in formal meditation but as a constant state.
  • केशव (keśava): O Krishna. A name for Krishna, often interpreted as ‘one with beautiful hair,’ or ‘the slayer of the Kesi demon,’ or ‘the controller of Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva,’ implying supreme divinity.
  • स्थितधीः (sthitadhīḥ): One of steady intellect (dhi means intellect or mind). This is a synonym for sthitaprajña, reinforcing the idea of a stable and unwavering mind.
  • किं (kiṁ): How, what.
  • प्रभाषेत (prabhāṣeta): Does he speak? (From the verbal root √bhāṣ, to speak).
  • किमासीत (kimāsīta): Does he sit? (From the verbal root √ās, to sit or remain).
  • व्रजेत (vrajeta): Does he walk? (From the verbal root √vraj, to walk, move, or act).
  • किम् (kim): (Again) What/how. This repetition emphasizes the comprehensive nature of Arjuna’s inquiry into their actions.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna has just expounded on the profound truths of the Self and the path of buddhi-yoga (the yoga of intellect) and karma-yoga (the yoga of action without attachment). Arjuna, a warrior facing a moral dilemma, naturally seeks to understand how these abstract principles translate into a living reality. He is asking for a practical blueprint, a tangible example of what it means to truly embody spiritual wisdom.

The term sthitaprajña is central here. It describes an individual whose prajñā (intuitive wisdom or discerning intellect) is sthita (firmly established). Such a person is not swayed by the dualities of life – pleasure and pain, gain and loss, honor and dishonor. Their wisdom is unshakeable, rooted in the understanding of the eternal Self and the transient nature of the material world. The addition of samādhistha further clarifies that this steadiness is not merely intellectual but deeply ingrained, a state of constant inner absorption in higher consciousness, even amidst worldly engagement.

Arjuna’s questions about how a sthitadhīḥ (another term for steady intellect) speaks, sits, and walks are highly significant. These three actions represent the complete spectrum of human existence and interaction with the world:

  • Speaking (प्रभाषेत - prabhāṣeta): This refers to verbal communication. Does a wise person speak truthfully, kindly, and beneficially? Is their speech free from attachment, anger, or ego? Does it reflect inner peace or agitation?
  • Sitting (आसीत - āsīta): This denotes a state of being, of resting, or remaining. It implies composure, inner stillness, and presence even when seemingly inactive. How does a wise person face solitude, challenges, or moments of inaction? Is there restlessness or profound calm?
  • Walking (व्रजेत - vrajeta): This encompasses all forms of action and engagement in the world. How does a wise person perform their duties, interact with others, and navigate life’s challenges? Are their actions driven by wisdom, detachment, and selfless purpose, or by selfish desires and impulses?

By asking about these fundamental aspects of human conduct – thought (implied by the state of intellect), word, and deed – Arjuna seeks a holistic understanding of a liberated being. He wants to know how spiritual realization translates into every aspect of daily life, providing a practical guide for aspirants. This section is a cornerstone of the Gita’s practical philosophy, moving beyond abstract concepts to the lived experience of spiritual realization.

Practical Application

  1. Mindful Self-Observation: Arjuna’s questions serve as a powerful prompt for us to observe our own conduct. Am I speaking impulsively or thoughtfully? Am I resting with a peaceful mind or with inner turmoil? Am I acting with presence and purpose, or am I constantly distracted? This practice of self-awareness is the crucial first step towards cultivating inner steadiness and aligning our actions with wisdom.
  2. Cultivating Thoughtful Responses: In moments of stress or strong emotion, instead of reacting immediately, we can pause and ask ourselves, “How would a person of steady intellect respond in this situation?” This conscious pause allows for wisdom to guide our speech and actions, rather than being driven by fleeting emotions or external pressures. It’s about choosing our response rather than simply reacting.
  3. Seeking and Emulating Role Models: Just as Arjuna sought a description to identify a sthitaprajña, we too can look for individuals in our lives, in history, or in spiritual traditions who exemplify wisdom, equanimity, and compassionate action. Observing their conduct, their words, and their inner calm can provide immense inspiration and a practical template for our own spiritual growth.

Reflection

  • How do my words, actions, and even my moments of stillness reflect my inner state? Am I congruent?
  • What are the key qualities I personally associate with a truly wise and peaceful individual?
  • How can I consciously bring more steadiness, mindfulness, and wisdom into my daily interactions and decisions, even in small ways?

Key Concepts

  • Sthitaprajña (Steady Wisdom): The ideal state of a spiritually realized individual whose intellect is firm, unwavering, and undisturbed by life’s dualities.
  • Samādhistha (Established in Samadhi): A state of being constantly absorbed in higher consciousness or spiritual equilibrium, not just during formal meditation, but amidst all worldly activities.
  • Holistic Conduct: The integration of inner spiritual wisdom with outer expression through all aspects of human life – thought, speech, and action.
  • Practical Spirituality: The emphasis on embodying spiritual truths and principles in one’s daily life, moving beyond mere intellectual understanding to lived experience.
