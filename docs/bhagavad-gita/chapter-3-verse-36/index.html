The Compelling Force of Sin: Arjuna's Inquiry

Arjuna asks Krishna what powerful force drives a person to commit wrong actions, even against their own will and better judgment.

June 19, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Yoga
3.36

Chapter 3: Karma Yoga

Verse 36

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

अर्जुन उवाच: अथ केन प्रयुक्तोऽयं पापं चरति पूरुषः | अनिच्छन्नपि वार्ष्णेय बलादिव नियोजितः || ३.३६ ||

Transliteration (IAST)

Arjuna uvāca: atha kena prayukto'yaṁ pāpaṁ carati pūruṣaḥ | anicchannapi vārṣṇeya balādiva niyojitaḥ || 3.36 ||

Translation

Arjuna said: O descendant of Vrishni (Krishna), what is it that impels a person to commit sin, even unwillingly, as if by force?

Explanation

Context

Chapter 3 of the Bhagavad Gita, known as Karma Yoga, focuses on the path of selfless action. Krishna has been elaborating on the necessity of performing one’s prescribed duties (dharma) without attachment to the fruits of labor, emphasizing that action is superior to inaction and essential for the maintenance of the world. He has explained how sacrifices (yajñas) sustain creation and how a wise person acts for the welfare of all, free from selfish desires. Krishna has also touched upon the hierarchy of control: the senses are superior to the body, the mind to the senses, the intellect to the mind, and the soul (Ātman) to the intellect. The ultimate instruction has been to conquer the powerful enemy of desire by controlling the senses, mind, and intellect, and realizing the true self.

At this juncture, Arjuna, having absorbed these profound teachings, raises a fundamental and deeply human question. He acknowledges the difficulty of adhering to righteous action, observing that people often commit acts they know are wrong, even when they wish not to. This verse marks a crucial turning point, as Arjuna seeks to understand the root cause of this internal compulsion, setting the stage for Krishna’s detailed explanation of desire (kāma) as the primary antagonist.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • अथ (atha): Now, then; introduces a new point or question.
  • केन (kena): By what? By whom? This interrogative highlights Arjuna’s search for the instigator.
  • प्रयुक्तोऽयम् (prayukto’yam): ‘Prayuktaḥ’ means impelled, prompted, driven, engaged. ‘Ayam’ means this (person).
  • पापं (pāpam): Sin; wrong action; that which leads to suffering or transgression against dharma.
  • चरति (carati): Performs, acts, commits, engages in.
  • पुरुषः (pūruṣaḥ): A person, a human being; emphasizing the universal nature of this struggle.
  • अनिच्छन्नपि (anicchannapi): ‘Anicchan’ means unwillingly, not wishing; ‘api’ means even. So, ‘even unwillingly’ or ‘against one’s wish’.
  • वार्ष्णेय (vārṣṇeya): O descendant of Vrishni; an appellation for Krishna, acknowledging his divine lineage and wisdom.
  • बलादिव (balādiva): ‘Balāt’ means by force, by compulsion; ‘iva’ means as if. So, ‘as if by force’.
  • नियोजितः (niyojitaḥ): Engaged, appointed, compelled. The sense is of being irresistibly directed.

Together, Arjuna is asking: “Now, by what is this person compelled to commit sin, even unwillingly, O Krishna, as if engaged by force?”

Deeper Understanding

Arjuna’s question is perhaps one of the most profound inquiries into human psychology and morality found in any spiritual text. He is articulating a universal human experience: the struggle between one’s higher intellect (buddhi), which understands right from wrong, and a powerful, often subconscious, lower impulse that drives one towards transgression. This isn’t merely about external temptation; it’s about an internal force that feels almost external in its power, compelling action against one’s conscious will.

Krishna’s teaching here will identify this compelling force as kāma (desire) and its offspring, krodha (anger). These are rooted in the rajo guṇa (the quality of passion and activity) of material nature. The Gita posits that the soul, in its pure state, is free and blissful. However, when entangled with the material energy (Prakriti), it becomes influenced by the guṇas. Desire, being a powerful manifestation of rajas, blinds the intellect and overpowers the will, making the individual act against their better judgment. It’s like an internal dictator, forcing the individual into actions they later regret.

This verse relates intimately to the themes of dharma (righteous duty), karma (action and its consequences), and yoga (union with the Divine). If one is compelled to act unrighteously, how can they truly perform their dharma? How can they achieve the state of equanimity and freedom from attachment that Krishna has been advocating? Arjuna’s question highlights the primary obstacle to achieving Karma Yoga: the internal enemy that prevents conscious, dharmic action. Krishna will reveal that true freedom lies not in suppressing desire, but in understanding its nature and transcending its grip through discriminative wisdom and self-control.

Practical Application

Arjuna’s question resonates deeply in contemporary life. We often find ourselves making resolutions, setting goals, or knowing what is good for us (e.g., eating healthy, exercising, avoiding procrastination), yet we succumb to impulses that lead us astray. This verse encourages us to investigate this internal compulsion:

  1. Identify the ‘Force’: Become a keen observer of your own mind. When you act against your better judgment (e.g., spending too much, procrastinating, indulging in unhealthy habits), pause and ask, “What is the underlying desire driving this?” Is it a desire for comfort, pleasure, avoidance of discomfort, validation, or something else? Naming the desire is the first step towards managing it.
  2. Create a Pause: The ‘as if by force’ suggests a lack of conscious choice. Practice mindfulness to create a space between the impulse and the action. Instead of immediately reacting to a desire (e.g., to check social media when you should be working), acknowledge the impulse without judgment, take a deep breath, and then consciously decide whether to act on it or not. This pause weakens the compulsive power.
  3. Strengthen the Intellect: Just as Krishna advised, the intellect is superior to the mind and senses. Continuously educate yourself, reflect on ethical principles, and reinforce your commitment to your higher values. When desires arise, consciously bring your intellect to bear: “Is this action aligned with my long-term goals? Is it truly beneficial?” This strengthens your inner resolve against the compelling force of desire.

Reflection

  • What specific actions or habits do I engage in, even when I know they are not good for me or go against my own values?
  • Can I identify the underlying desire or aversion that compels me in these moments?
  • How can I cultivate a greater sense of awareness and choice when these impulses arise?

Key Concepts

  • Internal Compulsion to Sin: The universal human experience of acting against one’s own will and better judgment.
  • The Nature of Desire (Kāma): The underlying force that drives individuals to transgression, often feeling irresistible.
  • Free Will vs. Impulse: The struggle between conscious, rational choice and powerful, often subconscious, urges.
  • Human Frailty: Acknowledging the inherent challenges in controlling the mind and senses, setting the stage for Krishna’s solution.
