The Cosmic Dance of Unmanifest and Manifest

Krishna explains that all beings emerge from and return to the unmanifest, existing only temporarily in a manifest state, thus rendering lamentation futile.

April 1, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 28

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

अव्यक्तादीनि भूतानि व्यक्तमध्यानि भारत। अव्यक्तनिधनान्येव तत्र का परिदेवना॥

Transliteration (IAST)

avyaktādīni bhūtāni vyaktamadhyāni bhārata। avyaktanidhanānyeva tatra kā paridevanā॥

Translation

All created beings are unmanifest before birth, manifest in the middle state, and unmanifest again after death, O Arjuna. What then is there to lament?

Explanation

Context

This profound verse from the Bhagavad Gita Chapter 2, Sankhya Yoga, continues Krishna’s systematic dismantling of Arjuna’s grief and confusion on the battlefield. Having previously established the eternal nature of the soul (Atman) and the temporary nature of the body, Krishna now extends this understanding to encompass all created beings. He is leading Arjuna to a broader, cosmic perspective, illustrating that the cycle of manifestation and dissolution is a universal law, not a personal tragedy. This teaching aims to remove the root cause of sorrow, which is attachment to the transient forms of existence.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • अव्यक्तादीनि (avyaktādīni): This compound term means ‘having unmanifest beginnings’. Avyakta refers to that which is unmanifest, unperceivable, or subtle, while ādīni denotes beginnings or origins. So, beings are in an unmanifest state before their physical birth.
  • भूतानि (bhūtāni): This refers to all created beings, entities, or elements – essentially, everything that comes into existence.
  • व्यक्तमध्यानि (vyaktamadhyāni): ‘Manifest in the middle state’. Vyakta means manifest, perceivable, or gross, and madhyāni refers to the middle or intermediate stage. This describes the current embodied existence, where beings are perceivable to our senses.
  • भारत (bhārata): O Arjuna, descendant of Bharata. This address signifies Arjuna’s noble lineage and wisdom, often used by Krishna to emphasize the importance of the teaching.
  • अव्यक्तनिधनान्येव (avyaktanidhanānyeva): ‘Unmanifest at their end, indeed’. Nidhanāni means ends or destructions, and eva is an emphatic particle, meaning ‘indeed’ or ‘certainly’. This signifies that after death, beings return to an unmanifest state.
  • तत्र (tatra): In that case, therefore.
  • का (kā): What?
  • परिदेवना (paridevanā): Lamentation, grief, sorrow, or wailing.

In essence, the verse states that all beings emerge from an unmanifest state, take on a manifest form for a period, and then return to an unmanifest state. Given this natural, universal cycle, Krishna questions the basis for lamentation.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s teaching in this verse is a cornerstone of Vedantic philosophy, offering a profound insight into the nature of reality and existence. He is guiding Arjuna, and by extension all seekers, to transcend the limited perception of the material world.

  • What is Krishna teaching Arjuna? Krishna is teaching Arjuna to look beyond the superficial, ever-changing forms of the material world and grasp the underlying, eternal truth. He emphasizes that sorrow arises from identifying with the temporary and manifest. By understanding that all forms are transient – appearing from the unmanifest and returning to it – one can free oneself from grief over their inevitable change or dissolution. The cycle of manifestation and unmanifestation is not an anomaly but a fundamental, natural rhythm of the cosmos.

  • Key concepts presented?

    • Impermanence (Anitya): The most direct teaching is the impermanence of all manifest entities. Every form, every body, every situation has a beginning, a middle, and an end. There is nothing in the material realm that is truly permanent.
    • Cyclical Nature of Existence: The universe operates in cycles. Creation and dissolution, birth and death, are not linear events but continuous processes. This cosmic dance of ‘coming into being’ and ‘returning to unbeing’ is inherent to Prakriti (primordial matter) and its manifestations.
    • The Unmanifest (Avyakta): This refers to the subtle, unperceivable state of existence. It’s not non-existence, but a state beyond our sensory and mental grasp. Before birth and after death, beings are in this subtle, unmanifest condition. Philosophically, it can relate to the causal state of Prakriti, or even the subtle body (linga sharira) carrying karmic imprints, awaiting a new manifestation.
    • The Futility of Lamentation: If everything is subject to this natural cycle, then lamenting over change or loss is based on a misunderstanding of reality. Grief arises from attachment to the temporary, which is bound to cause suffering when that temporary form inevitably changes or ceases.

  • How does this relate to dharma, karma, yoga, or other Gita themes?

    • Dharma: This understanding is crucial for performing one’s dharma (righteous duty) without emotional entanglement. If Arjuna grasps that his relatives’ bodies are temporary, his grief will subside, enabling him to fulfill his duty as a warrior without personal attachment to outcomes.
    • Yoga: This verse is foundational for Jnana Yoga (the path of knowledge), which involves discriminating between the real (eternal) and the unreal (temporary). It also supports Karma Yoga, as actions can be performed with detachment from results, knowing that all manifest outcomes are transient. Understanding the ‘Avyakta’ also connects to meditation practices aimed at transcending the manifest world.
    • Karma: The soul’s journey through various lives (samsara) is an embodiment of this verse. The soul, carrying its karmic impressions, moves from an unmanifest state (between lives) to a manifest state (a new body) and back again.
    • Non-attachment (Vairagya): The verse directly promotes non-attachment to the physical world and its transient phenomena. Vairagya, or dispassion, is a key virtue in spiritual life, freeing one from the suffering caused by clinging to impermanent things.

Practical Application

  1. Coping with Loss and Change: In modern life, we constantly face loss—of loved ones, jobs, relationships, health, or even youth. This teaching provides a profound framework for understanding and processing such experiences. By realizing that all forms are temporary manifestations, we can shift our perspective from ‘permanent loss’ to ‘natural transformation.’ This doesn’t mean suppressing grief, but understanding its context within the larger cosmic cycle, allowing for acceptance and ultimately, peace.
  2. Embracing Impermanence in Daily Life: We often resist change, clinging to comfort zones or past glories. This verse encourages us to observe and accept the impermanent nature of everything around us – a beautiful flower wilting, a technology becoming obsolete, a mood shifting. By embracing this truth, we cultivate adaptability and resilience, reducing anxiety about the future and fostering a more present-moment awareness. We learn to appreciate things while they are manifest, without undue attachment to their permanence.
  3. Reducing Anxiety about Outcomes: Many struggles stem from attachment to specific outcomes in our endeavors. Whether it’s a project at work, a personal goal, or a relationship, fear of failure or disappointment can be paralyzing. By internalizing that all manifest results are temporary and part of a larger, evolving process, we can focus on our efforts (karma) with dedication, while remaining detached from the ultimate, transient outcome. This detachment fosters inner peace and allows for greater creativity and spontaneity.

Reflection

  • How does my current perception of ‘permanence’ contribute to my suffering or attachment?
  • Can I observe the cycles of birth, growth, decay, and dissolution in nature and in my own life without emotional entanglement?
  • What would it mean to live with the understanding that all manifest things are essentially temporary appearances, and how might that change my actions and reactions?

Key Concepts

  • Impermanence of Forms (Anitya): All manifest entities, including bodies and situations, have a temporary existence.
  • Cyclical Nature of Existence: The universe operates in continuous cycles of manifestation and dissolution.
  • The Unmanifest (Avyakta): The subtle, unperceivable state from which things emerge and to which they return.
  • Detachment (Vairagya): Freedom from sorrow and attachment by understanding the transient nature of the material world.
