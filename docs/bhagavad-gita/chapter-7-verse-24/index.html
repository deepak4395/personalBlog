The Illusion of Form: Understanding Krishna's Unmanifest Reality

Krishna explains that ignorant individuals mistake His manifest form for His ultimate, unchangeable, supreme nature, failing to grasp His true divinity.

November 15, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Jnana Vijnana Yoga
7.24

Chapter 7: Jnana Vijnana Yoga

Verse 24

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

अव्यक्तं व्यक्तिमापन्नं मन्यन्ते मामबुद्धयः। परं भावमजानन्तो ममाव्ययमनुत्तमम्॥

Transliteration (IAST)

avyaktaṁ vyaktim āpannaṁ manyante mām abuddhayaḥ | paraṁ bhāvam ajānanto mamāvyayam anuttamam ||

Translation

Unintelligent men, unaware of My higher nature which is immutable and supreme, think that I, the unmanifest, have assumed a manifest form.

Explanation

Context

Chapter 7 of the Bhagavad Gita, titled ‘Jnana Vijnana Yoga’ (The Yoga of Knowledge and Realized Knowledge), is a profound discourse where Lord Krishna reveals the depth and breadth of His divine nature to Arjuna. Having previously explained His dual energies (the lower material energy and the higher spiritual energy) and asserted that He is the origin and dissolution of all creation, Krishna now addresses a common misconception. This verse directly tackles the limited understanding of those who perceive Him merely through His temporary, manifest forms, failing to grasp His eternal, transcendental essence. It’s a crucial teaching that distinguishes between a superficial, material understanding of the divine and a deep, spiritual realization.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • अव्यक्तं (avyaktaṁ): Unmanifest, invisible, imperceptible. This refers to Krishna’s true, transcendental form, which is beyond material senses.
  • व्यक्तिमापन्नं (vyaktim āpannaṁ): Having attained manifestation; having become manifest or embodied. This refers to His appearance in a perceivable form, such as His human-like form as Krishna on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, or any other divine incarnation.
  • मन्यन्ते (manyante): They think, they consider, they believe.
  • माम् (mām): Me (referring to Lord Krishna).
  • अबुद्धयः (abuddhayaḥ): Unintelligent persons; those without proper understanding or spiritual intelligence. This implies a lack of discerning wisdom.
  • परं भावम् (paraṁ bhāvam): Higher nature, supreme existence, transcendental state. This is Krishna’s ultimate, spiritual reality.
  • अजानन्तः (ajānantaḥ): Not knowing, unaware of, ignorant of.
  • मम (mama): My.
  • अव्ययम् (avyayam): Immutable, imperishable, eternal, unchanging.
  • अनुत्तमम् (anuttamam): Unsurpassable, supreme, unequalled, best.

Combined, the verse states that those lacking true spiritual intelligence mistake Krishna’s manifest form as His ultimate reality because they are unaware of His higher, immutable, and supreme nature.

Deeper Understanding

In this pivotal verse, Krishna directly confronts the limitations of human perception and intellect when attempting to comprehend the Absolute Truth. He makes a clear distinction between His avyakta (unmanifest, eternal) nature and His vyakta (manifest, perceivable) forms. The ‘unintelligent men’ (abuddhayaḥ) are those who, confined by their sensory experiences and material reasoning, perceive Krishna only as a historical figure, a powerful king, or a mere mortal, even when He displays divine powers. They fail to understand that His appearance in a manifest form is a voluntary act, a divine sport (lila), and not a transformation of His inherent, unchangeable essence.

Krishna’s true nature, described as paraṁ bhāvam (supreme existence), avyayam (immutable), and anuttamam (unsurpassable), signifies His eternal, transcendental, and all-pervading reality. He is not born, nor does He die; He is not subject to the cycles of time and matter. When He appears, He does so in His original spiritual form, which is never tainted by material nature. The mistake of the ‘abuddhayaḥ’ lies in projecting material limitations onto the divine, assuming that just as a human being takes birth and dies, so too must Krishna be bound by such constraints.

This teaching is fundamental to understanding the nature of God in the Bhagavad Gita and various Vedic traditions. It highlights that the divine is not confined by any single form, although it can manifest in countless forms. The ultimate reality transcends all forms and attributes, yet it is simultaneously the source and sustainer of all forms. This concept relates deeply to the theme of Maya, the divine illusory energy that veils the true nature of the Lord from those who are not spiritually enlightened. Without jnana (true knowledge) and vijnana (realized knowledge), one remains trapped in a superficial understanding, mistaking the temporary for the eternal.

Practical Application

  1. Look Beyond the Surface: In daily life, we often make judgments based on superficial appearances – be it people, situations, or even spiritual concepts. This verse encourages us to cultivate deeper insight, to look for the underlying essence, the unmanifest truth behind the manifest form. For instance, in relationships, seeing beyond temporary moods or actions to the inherent good in a person. In challenges, looking beyond the immediate difficulty to the potential for growth and learning.
  2. Cultivate Spiritual Discernment (Buddhi): The Gita consistently emphasizes the importance of buddhi, or higher intelligence. This verse teaches us to use our intellect to distinguish between the temporary and the eternal, the material and the spiritual. This means not accepting things at face value but seeking deeper understanding through study, contemplation, and introspection. It applies to understanding spiritual texts, but also to making wise choices in our careers, relationships, and personal development, always seeking what is truly beneficial and lasting.
  3. Embrace Humility in Understanding: Recognizing that our human perception and intellect are inherently limited when it comes to ultimate truths fosters humility. This prevents dogmatism and encourages an open mind to continuous learning and spiritual growth. Instead of asserting what God must be, one can remain open to the myriad ways the divine can manifest and be understood, always striving for a deeper, more comprehensive realization.

Reflection

  • What preconceived notions do I hold about the divine or ultimate reality, and how might they limit my understanding?
  • How often do I judge people or situations based solely on their external appearance or manifest form, rather than seeking a deeper understanding of their true nature?
  • What practices can I adopt to cultivate greater spiritual discernment and look beyond the superficial in my life?

Key Concepts

  1. Avyakta and Vyakta (Unmanifest and Manifest): The fundamental distinction between Krishna’s eternal, imperceptible essence and His temporary, perceivable forms or incarnations.
  2. Param Bhavam (Supreme Nature): Krishna’s ultimate, transcendental, immutable, and unsurpassable reality, which is beyond all material limitations.
  3. Abuddhayaḥ (Unintelligent): Refers to those whose limited material perception and intellect prevent them from grasping the true, transcendental nature of the divine.
  4. Spiritual Ignorance: The inability to discern the eternal from the temporary, leading to a misidentification of the divine’s true essence with its manifest forms.
