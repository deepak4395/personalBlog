Mastering the Mind: Practice and Detachment (Bhagavad Gita 6.35)

Krishna confirms the mind's inherent restlessness but offers a powerful, dual solution: disciplined practice (abhyasa) and dispassion (vairagya) can control it.

October 10, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Atma Samyama Yoga
6.35

Chapter 6: Atma Samyama Yoga

Verse 35

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

असंशयं महाबाहो मनो दुर्निग्रहं चलम्। अभ्यासेन तु कौन्तेय वैराग्येण च गृह्यते।।35।।

Transliteration (IAST)

asaṃśayaṃ mahābāho mano durnigrahaṃ calam | abhyāsena tu kaunteya vairāgyeṇa ca gṛhyate ||35||

Translation

Lord Krishna said: Undoubtedly, O mighty-armed Arjuna, the mind is restless and difficult to curb. But, O son of Kunti, it can be controlled by constant practice and detachment.

Explanation

Context

In Chapter 6, titled ‘Atma Samyama Yoga’ (The Yoga of Self-Control), Lord Krishna has been guiding Arjuna on the path of meditation and inner discipline. Arjuna, a warrior facing the greatest moral dilemma of his life, naturally finds the prospect of controlling his mind daunting. In the preceding verse (6.34), he frankly admits his struggle, comparing the mind’s untamable nature to that of the wind – ‘cañcalaṁ hi manaḥ kṛṣṇa pramāthi balavad dṛḍham | tasyāhaṁ nigrahaṁ manye vāyor iva suduṣkaram ||’ (The mind, O Krishna, is restless, turbulent, strong, and obstinate; I think it is as difficult to control as the wind). Krishna, ever the compassionate and practical teacher, acknowledges Arjuna’s genuine concern in this verse (6.35). He validates the difficulty but immediately offers a clear, actionable solution, providing hope and a definite path forward for anyone grappling with an unruly mind.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • असंशयं (asaṃśayaṃ): Undoubtedly; without a doubt. Krishna begins by affirming Arjuna’s observation.
  • महाबाहो (mahābāho): O mighty-armed one. An epithet for Arjuna, signifying his strength and prowess, yet implying that even a warrior of his caliber finds the mind a formidable opponent.
  • मनो (mano): The mind.
  • दुर्निग्रहं (durnigrahaṃ): Difficult to curb, restrain, or control. This confirms the mind’s stubborn nature.
  • चलम् (calam): Restless, unsteady, fickle, wavering. This describes the inherent quality of the mind.
  • अभ्यासेन (abhyāsena): By practice; through constant and repeated effort. This is the first key solution.
  • तु (tu): But; however. This marks the transition from acknowledging the problem to presenting the solution.
  • कौन्तेय (kaunteya): O son of Kunti. Another name for Arjuna.
  • वैराग्येण (vairāgyeṇa): By detachment; through dispassion, non-attachment, or renunciation of desires. This is the second key solution.
  • च (ca): And.
  • गृह्यते (gṛhyate): Is controlled; is grasped, restrained, or brought under command.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s response in 6.35 is profound because it first validates the universal human experience of a restless mind and then offers a timeless, two-pronged strategy for its mastery. He doesn’t dismiss Arjuna’s frustration but rather acknowledges the mind’s inherent calam (restlessness) and durnigrahaṃ (difficulty to control).

The solution lies in the twin pillars of Abhyasa (constant practice) and Vairagya (detachment). These two concepts are not unique to the Gita; they are fundamental to various Indian spiritual traditions, most notably Patanjali’s Yoga Sutras (1.12), which states: abhyāsa-vairāgyābhyāṁ tan-nirodhaḥ (By practice and detachment, the modifications of the mind are restrained).

Abhyasa refers to consistent, sustained effort. It’s the discipline of repeatedly bringing the mind back to a chosen object of focus – be it a mantra, the breath, a divine form, or a particular thought. It’s not about achieving perfection in a single sitting but about the continuous process of trying, failing, and trying again. Abhyasa builds new neural pathways, strengthens concentration, and gradually diminishes the mind’s tendency to wander. It’s the active training of the mind, much like physical exercise trains the body.

Vairagya is dispassion or non-attachment. It’s not about renouncing the world or becoming emotionless, but about cultivating an inner freedom from craving (raga) and aversion (dvesha) towards sensory objects, experiences, and outcomes. Vairagya helps us see things as they are, without the distorting lens of desire or fear. It’s the wisdom that understands the impermanence and ultimately unsatisfying nature of external pursuits when sought as the sole source of happiness. When the mind is detached from the outcomes of actions and the allure of fleeting pleasures, its restless energy naturally subsides.

These two principles work synergistically. Abhyasa provides the positive discipline, while Vairagya removes the distractions and pulls that make the mind restless. Practice without detachment can lead to frustration or even attachment to the practice itself. Detachment without practice might become passive indifference. Together, they create a powerful dynamic for inner transformation, leading the individual towards a state of mental equilibrium and inner peace, which is the very essence of Yoga.

Practical Application

  1. Consistent Mindfulness/Meditation Practice: Integrate short, regular periods of mindfulness or meditation into your daily routine. Even 10-15 minutes of focused breathing, observing thoughts without judgment, or chanting can be a powerful abhyasa. The key is consistency, not duration.
  2. Digital Detox & Conscious Consumption: Practice vairagya by consciously detaching from constant digital stimulation, social media validation, or the relentless pursuit of material possessions. Set specific times for checking devices, curate your information intake, and question the true necessity of new purchases. This cultivates dispassion towards external distractions.
  3. Process-Oriented Approach to Goals: When pursuing goals (career, fitness, learning), apply abhyasa by focusing on the consistent effort and daily steps. Simultaneously, cultivate vairagya by detaching from the immediate outcome or the desire for perfect results. Understand that true growth comes from the process, not just the destination, reducing anxiety and mental agitation related to success or failure.

Reflection

  • What specific areas of my life reveal the mind’s restlessness most clearly? How can I apply abhyasa and vairagya to these areas?
  • Am I consistently engaging in any form of ‘practice’ for my mind? If not, what small step can I take today?
  • Where can I cultivate more detachment from outcomes, opinions, or desires that cause me mental agitation?

Key Concepts

  • Mind’s Restlessness (Calam): Acknowledgment of the mind’s inherent fluctuating and unsteady nature.
  • Abhyasa (Constant Practice): The discipline of sustained, repeated effort to focus and train the mind.
  • Vairagya (Detachment/Dispassion): Cultivating non-attachment to sensory objects, desires, and outcomes.
  • Mano Nigraha (Mind Control): The ultimate goal of bringing the mind under conscious command through the synergy of practice and detachment.
