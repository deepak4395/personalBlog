The Cosmic Cycle: From Action to Sustenance (Bhagavad Gita 3.14)

This verse reveals the interconnected cycle of food, rain, sacrifice, and action, highlighting humanity's role in cosmic interdependence.

May 29, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Yoga #Yajna #Interdependence #Duty #Sustenance
3.14

Chapter 3: Karma Yoga

Verse 14

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

अन्नाद्भवन्ति भूतानि पर्जन्यादन्नसम्भवः। यज्ञाद्भवति पर्जन्यो यज्ञः कर्मसमुद्भवः॥१४॥

Transliteration (IAST)

annād bhavanti bhūtāni parjanyād anna-sambhavaḥ | yajñād bhavati parjanyo yajñaḥ karma-samudbhavaḥ || 14 ||

Translation

From food, all beings are born; from rain, food is produced; from sacrifice, rain ensues; and sacrifice is born of action.

Explanation

Context

In the third chapter of the Bhagavad Gita, titled “Karma Yoga,” Lord Krishna is guiding Arjuna on the path of selfless action. Having established in previous verses (3.8-3.13) that action is inevitable and necessary, and that actions performed as a form of ‘yajña’ (sacrifice) do not bind one to karma, Krishna now elaborates on the profound cosmic interconnectedness that underpins all existence. This verse, 3.14, delves into the intricate cycle of sustenance, illustrating how human action, particularly in the spirit of sacrifice, is an integral part of maintaining the very fabric of life. It moves from the tangible (beings, food, rain) to the subtle (sacrifice, action), demonstrating that our physical well-being is deeply entwined with our spiritual responsibilities.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • अन्नाद् (annād): From food, from nourishment.
  • भवन्ति (bhavanti): Are born, come into being.
  • भूतानि (bhūtāni): All beings, living creatures.
  • पर्जन्याद् (parjanyād): From rain, from the cloud-god Parjanya.
  • अन्नसम्भवः (anna-sambhavaḥ): Food’s production, the possibility of food.
  • यज्ञाद् (yajñād): From sacrifice, from ritual or selfless offering.
  • भवति (bhavati): Ensures, happens.
  • पर्जन्यः (parjanyaḥ): Rain.
  • यज्ञः (yajñaḥ): Sacrifice.
  • कर्मसमुद्भवः (karma-samudbhavaḥ): Born of action, originating from action.

Deeper Understanding

This verse articulates a fundamental principle of cosmic order, often referred to as the ‘karma-chakra’ or the ‘wheel of action and sustenance.’ Krishna presents a chain of cause and effect that sustains all life:

  1. Food sustains life: All living beings depend on food for their existence and growth. This is the most immediate and observable truth.
  2. Rain produces food: The availability of food, especially grains and crops, is directly dependent on adequate rainfall. This highlights the role of natural elements and divine provision.
  3. Sacrifice brings rain: Here lies the crucial link where human action intersects with cosmic processes. ‘Yajña’ (sacrifice) in this context is not merely a ritualistic offering into fire. While it includes such Vedic rituals, its deeper meaning encompasses any selfless action, performed with a spirit of offering to the divine or for the welfare of the world, without attachment to the results. Such actions are believed to harmonize with the cosmic forces, leading to favorable conditions like rain.
  4. Sacrifice is born of action: Ultimately, ‘yajña’ itself is an action, a conscious effort. It requires intention, effort, and dedication. This brings us back to Karma Yoga – the performance of duty without selfish motives.

Krishna is teaching Arjuna that life is not a series of isolated events but an intricate web of interdependence. No being can exist in isolation, and human actions have far-reaching consequences, influencing not just our immediate environment but the very forces of nature that sustain us. The concept of ‘yajña’ elevates ordinary work into a spiritual practice. When our actions are performed as an offering, as a contribution to the larger whole, they become sacred and contribute to the cosmic balance, ensuring the flow of sustenance. This moves beyond a materialistic view of life, suggesting that true prosperity and well-being are rooted in a spirit of giving and selfless duty.

Practical Application

  1. Conscious Consumption and Gratitude: Recognize that every meal is a product of this vast cosmic cycle, involving rain, soil, sunlight, and the labor of many. Practice gratitude before eating, acknowledging the interconnectedness. This can lead to more mindful eating habits, reduced waste, and support for sustainable food systems.
  2. Transforming Work into Service: View your daily work, whether as a professional, a parent, or a community member, as a form of ‘yajña.’ Instead of merely working for personal gain, approach your tasks with a spirit of offering and contribution to the greater good. A teacher educating students, a doctor healing the sick, an engineer building infrastructure – all can be acts of ‘sacrifice’ that contribute to societal well-being, metaphorically bringing ‘rain’ to the community.
  3. Environmental Stewardship: Understand our direct responsibility in maintaining the ecological balance. Pollution, deforestation, and over-exploitation disrupt the natural cycle (e.g., affecting rainfall patterns). Engaging in environmentally conscious actions, supporting conservation, and advocating for sustainable practices are modern forms of ‘yajña’ that help ensure the continuity of the life-sustaining cycle.

Reflection

  • How do my daily actions contribute to or detract from the well-being of the larger world around me?
  • Can I identify areas in my life where I consume without giving back, and how can I shift towards a more balanced approach?
  • What specific actions can I take to transform my work or daily activities into acts of selfless contribution?

Key Concepts

  • Cosmic Interdependence: All elements of creation are interconnected and mutually dependent.
  • The Cycle of Sustenance (Karma-Chakra): A continuous loop where actions lead to consequences that sustain life, emphasizing human responsibility.
  • Yajña as Selfless Action/Duty: ‘Sacrifice’ as performing one’s duties and actions with a spirit of offering for the welfare of others or the divine, rather than for personal gain.
  • Karma as the Originator of Sacrifice: All forms of ‘yajña’ ultimately stem from conscious, intentional action, highlighting the importance of righteous karma.
