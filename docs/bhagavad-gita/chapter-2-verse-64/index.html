The Path to Tranquility: Mastering Senses and Emotions

A self-controlled individual, engaging with sense objects without attachment or aversion, and with senses under command, achieves profound inner peace and tranquility.

May 7, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga
2.64

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 64

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

रागद्वेषविमुक्तैस्तु विषयानिन्द्रियैश्चरन्। आत्मवश्यैर्विधेयात्मा प्रसादमधिगच्छति॥

Transliteration (IAST)

rāga-dveṣa-vimuktais tu viṣayān indriyaiś caran | ātmavaśyair vidheyātmā prasādam adhigacchati ||

Translation

But a disciplined soul, moving among sense objects with the senses free from attachment and aversion and under his control, attains tranquility.

Explanation

Context

Verse 2.64 is a pivotal teaching within the Bhagavad Gita’s second chapter, ‘Sankhya Yoga,’ where Lord Krishna is meticulously describing the characteristics and path of a sthitaprajña – a person of steady wisdom. This chapter serves as the philosophical bedrock for the entire Gita, laying out the principles of discerning reality from illusion, the nature of the self, and the importance of self-control.

Before this verse, Krishna systematically outlined the perilous journey of an uncontrolled mind: dwelling on sense objects leads to attachment (saṅga), attachment breeds desire (kāma), unfulfilled desire turns into anger (krodha), anger causes delusion (sammoha), delusion impairs memory (smṛti-bhraṁśa), loss of memory destroys intelligence (buddhi-nāśa), and the destruction of intelligence leads to utter ruin (praṇaśyati). Verse 63 culminated this warning. Verse 64, therefore, presents the antidote, the alternative, and the path to liberation from this cycle of psychological downfall. It offers a powerful contrast, showing how one can engage with the world without being entangled by it, thus achieving true inner peace.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • रागद्वेषविमुक्तैः (rāga-dveṣa-vimuktaiḥ): ‘Free from attachment and aversion.’ This is the core. rāga refers to desire, attraction, attachment, or liking, while dveṣa signifies aversion, repulsion, or dislike. vimuktaiḥ means ‘freed from’ or ‘liberated from.’ This phrase emphasizes that the senses themselves are not the problem, but rather our emotional conditioning towards them.
  • तु (tu): ‘But’ or ‘indeed.’ This conjunction introduces a contrasting idea to the preceding verses about the perils of uncontrolled senses.
  • विषयान् (viṣayān): ‘Sense objects.’ These are the stimuli that our senses perceive – sights, sounds, tastes, smells, and textures.
  • इन्द्रियैः (indriyaiḥ): ‘By means of the senses.’ It refers to the five organs of perception (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin) and the mind.
  • चरन् (caran): ‘Moving,’ ‘acting,’ ‘dwelling,’ ‘engaging.’ It implies interacting with the world and its objects.
  • आत्मवश्यैः (ātmavaśyaiḥ): ‘Under one’s own control’ or ‘controlled by the self.’ This refers to the senses being disciplined and not running wild, directed by the intellect rather than their own impulses.
  • विधेयात्मा (vidheyātmā): ‘A disciplined soul’ or ‘one whose mind is obedient.’ vidheya means obedient or amenable, and ātmā here refers to the mind or the self. It describes a person whose inner being is well-governed and self-regulated.
  • प्रसादम् (prasādam): ‘Tranquility,’ ‘serenity,’ ‘grace,’ ‘clearness,’ ‘purity of mind.’ This is the state of profound inner peace and mental clarity that is attained.
  • अधिगच्छति (adhigacchati): ‘Attains,’ ‘achieves,’ ‘reaches.’

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s teaching here is profoundly liberating. He is not advocating for ascetic withdrawal from the world, which might be impossible or impractical for many. Instead, He offers a practical path of detached engagement. The key insight is that the problem isn’t the sense objects themselves, nor even the senses, but our relationship with them – specifically, the dualistic pull of rāga (attachment) and dveṣa (aversion).

When our senses interact with the world, if they are conditioned by rāga (e.g., craving a certain taste, longing for a particular sight), we become enslaved. Similarly, if they are driven by dveṣa (e.g., hating a sound, avoiding an unpleasant situation), we are equally bound. Both rāga and dveṣa disturb our inner equilibrium, creating a constant state of agitation.

Krishna explains that a vidheyātmā – a disciplined soul – can engage with viṣayān (sense objects) through their indriyaiḥ (senses). The crucial difference is that these senses are ātmavaśyaiḥ (under one’s own control) and rāga-dveṣa-vimuktaiḥ (free from attachment and aversion). This means one experiences the world consciously, without the emotional baggage of craving or repulsion. The senses become tools for experiencing, not masters dictating one’s inner state.

This state of self-mastery leads to prasādam – a profound inner tranquility. Prasādam is more than just the absence of disturbance; it is a positive state of mental clarity, serenity, and inner grace. It signifies a mind that is pure, calm, and poised, capable of clear perception and decision-making, unaffected by external ups and downs. This directly relates to karma yoga, where actions are performed without attachment to their fruits, and to dharma, as living a life of balance and righteousness naturally fosters such inner peace.

Practical Application

  1. Mindful Engagement with Media: When consuming news, social media, or entertainment, observe your internal reactions. Are you getting drawn into anger, frustration (dveṣa), or excessive excitement and craving for more (rāga)? Practice consuming information neutrally, absorbing what’s useful without letting the content dictate your emotional state. This allows you to stay informed without becoming emotionally hijacked.
  2. Eating with Awareness: During meals, instead of mindlessly indulging in what you ‘like’ (rāga) or avoiding what you ‘dislike’ (dveṣa), try to eat mindfully. Focus on the taste, texture, and nourishment. Notice if your senses are driving you to overeat or to reject healthy options based purely on conditioned preferences. This practice helps bring the sense of taste under conscious control.
  3. Responding to Criticism or Praise: In professional or personal interactions, when you receive feedback, whether positive or negative, observe your immediate emotional response. Do you swell with pride (rāga) or shrink in defensiveness (dveṣa)? Practice acknowledging the feedback calmly, evaluating it objectively, and responding thoughtfully rather than reacting emotionally. This strengthens your ātmavaśyaiḥ (self-control) over your emotional senses.

  • What does the experience of prasādam feel like for me, and what steps can I take to invite more of it into my life?

Key Concepts

  1. Freedom from Attachment and Aversion (rāga-dveṣa-vimuktaiḥ): The core teaching that true peace comes from releasing our emotional dependence on pleasant or unpleasant experiences.
  2. Sense Control (indriyaiś ātmavaśyaiḥ): The necessity of bringing the senses under the discipline of the intellect, making them instruments rather than masters.
  3. Disciplined Mind (vidheyātmā): The characteristic of a person whose inner self is well-governed, obedient to higher wisdom, and not swayed by external stimuli.
  4. Inner Tranquility (prasādam): The ultimate attainment, a state of profound mental clarity, serenity, and peace that arises from self-mastery.
