The Vision of Oneness: Seeing Divinity in All Beings

The wise, endowed with true knowledge and humility, perceive the same divine essence in all beings, from a learned sage to an outcaste, a cow to a dog.

August 25, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Sanyasa Yoga
5.18

Chapter 5: Karma Sanyasa Yoga

Verse 18

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

विद्याविनयसम्पन्ने ब्राह्मणे गवि हस्तिनि। शुनि चैव श्वपाके च पण्डिताः समदर्शिनः॥

Transliteration (IAST)

vidyāvinayasampanne brāhmaṇe gavi hastini | śuni caiva śvapāke ca paṇḍitāḥ samadarśinaḥ ||

Translation

The wise, endowed with true knowledge and humility, see with equal vision a brāhmaṇa, a cow, an elephant, a dog, and a dog-eater (outcaste).

Explanation

Context

Chapter 5 of the Bhagavad Gita, ‘Karma Sanyasa Yoga,’ focuses on reconciling the path of action (Karma Yoga) with the path of renunciation (Sannyasa). Krishna explains that true renunciation is not the abandonment of action itself, but the renunciation of attachment to the fruits of action and the sense of doership. He emphasizes that both paths lead to the same goal when performed with the right understanding and attitude. This particular verse (5.18) comes after Krishna has described the characteristics of a wise person (pandita) who is free from ego, attachment, and who finds joy within. It sets the stage for understanding the profound equanimity that such an enlightened being possesses, laying the philosophical groundwork for seeing the divine in everything and everyone, a core tenet of spiritual realization.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • विद्याविनयसम्पन्ने (vidyāvinayasampanne): ‘Endowed with knowledge and humility.’ Vidyā refers to spiritual wisdom or discriminative knowledge, not mere intellectual learning. Vinaya signifies humility, modesty, and disciplined conduct. The combination suggests that true wisdom is always accompanied by humility, not arrogance.
  • ब्राह्मणे (brāhmaṇe): ‘In a brāhmaṇa.’ Traditionally, a brāhmaṇa represents the highest social and spiritual class, revered for their learning and purity.
  • गवि (gavi): ‘In a cow.’ The cow is considered sacred and a symbol of nourishment and purity in Indian culture.
  • हस्तिनि (hastini): ‘In an elephant.’ The elephant is a powerful and majestic animal.
  • शुनि (śuni): ‘In a dog.’ The dog, in ancient Indian society, was often associated with impurity and was not held in high regard.
  • चैव (caiva): ‘And also.’
  • श्वपाके (śvapāke): ‘In a dog-eater.’ This refers to an outcaste or a person of the lowest social strata, traditionally considered extremely impure and marginalized.
  • च (ca): ‘And.’
  • पण्डिताः (paṇḍitāḥ): ‘The wise,’ ‘the learned,’ ‘the enlightened ones.’ These are individuals who have transcended superficial understanding and possess deep spiritual insight.
  • समदर्शिनः (samadarśinaḥ): ‘Those who see with equal vision,’ ‘equanimous.’ This is the core concept – perceiving an underlying unity despite external differences.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna is teaching Arjuna a profound truth about the nature of reality and the vision of an enlightened being. The key concept here is samadarśana, or equal vision. A truly wise person, a pandita, sees beyond the external forms, social distinctions, or species differences. They recognize the same divine essence, the Ātman or Brahman, residing within all beings. Whether it is a highly revered brāhmaṇa, a sacred cow, a mighty elephant, a common dog, or a marginalized dog-eater, the wise perceive the immutable, eternal Spirit as equally present and shining within them all.

This isn’t about treating everyone identically in a practical sense (e.g., interacting with a dog the same way one interacts with a human). Rather, it’s about perceiving the intrinsic worth and spiritual equality of all beings at a fundamental level. The examples chosen by Krishna are deliberate, spanning the extremes of social hierarchy and different species, to emphasize the radical and universal nature of this equal vision. It challenges all forms of prejudice, discrimination, and superficial judgment. This perspective arises from true knowledge (vidyā) coupled with humility (vinaya), as the ego-driven mind, which creates distinctions and hierarchies, has been subdued. This equanimity is a direct result of realizing the oneness of the Self with the Supreme Self, a central theme in the Gita and Vedanta philosophy. It liberates one from the cycles of attachment and aversion, leading to a state of profound inner peace and spiritual freedom.

Practical Application

  1. Overcoming Prejudice and Bias: In our diverse and often divided world, this verse is a powerful call to dismantle prejudice. We can apply this by consciously challenging our own biases against individuals or groups based on their race, religion, nationality, socio-economic status, or even personal preferences. Recognizing the shared humanity and divine spark in everyone can foster greater understanding and compassion.
  2. Cultivating Empathy for All Life: Extend this equal vision to all living beings. Understanding that animals, too, possess consciousness and a divine spark can inspire greater kindness towards them, leading to more ethical choices in diet, consumerism, and environmental stewardship. It encourages us to see ourselves as part of a larger, interconnected web of life.
  3. Achieving Inner Peace through Non-Judgment: Much of our mental turmoil stems from judging others, comparing ourselves, and forming attachments or aversions based on external appearances. By cultivating samadarśana, we train our minds to look beyond superficial differences, reducing the mental agitation caused by constant evaluation. This practice fosters a more stable, peaceful, and accepting state of mind, allowing us to interact with the world with greater equanimity.

Reflection

  • How often do I judge others based on their external appearance, social status, or background?
  • Can I consciously seek to recognize the inherent worth and potential divinity in every person I encounter today, regardless of how they present themselves?
  • What specific actions can I take to cultivate a more inclusive and less judgmental perspective in my daily life?

Key Concepts

  1. Samadarśana (Equal Vision): The profound spiritual insight to perceive the same divine essence (Ātman/Brahman) equally present in all beings, transcending all superficial distinctions of form, species, or social status.
  2. Vidyāvinayasampanne (Knowledge with Humility): True spiritual wisdom is inseparable from humility. It’s not mere intellectual understanding but a humble realization of the universal Spirit, which eradicates ego and fosters a non-judgmental perspective.
  3. Unity of Ātman and Brahman: The underlying philosophical principle that the individual soul (Ātman) is identical with the universal Spirit (Brahman), and this Spirit pervades all existence. Realizing this oneness is the basis of equal vision.
  4. Transcendence of Dualities: The enlightened state involves moving beyond the relative pairs of opposites (pure/impure, high/low, good/bad) that the mind creates, understanding them as temporary manifestations compared to the eternal, unchanging reality.
