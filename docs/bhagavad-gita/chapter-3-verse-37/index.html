Unmasking the Inner Adversary: Desire and Anger

Krishna reveals that desire (kama) and anger (krodha), born of rajas, are insatiable, destructive forces and our greatest enemies.

June 20, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Karma Yoga #Desire #Anger #Rajas #Self-Control
3.37

Chapter 3: Karma Yoga

Verse 37

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

श्रीभगवानुवाच काम एष क्रोध एष रजोगुणसमुद्भवः। महाशनो महापाप्मा विद्ध्येनमिह वैरिणम्।।3.37।।

Transliteration (IAST)

śrībhagavānuvāca kāma eṣa krodha eṣa rajoguṇasamudbhavaḥ। mahāśano mahāpāpmā viddhyenamiha vairiṇam।।3.37।।

Translation

The Blessed Lord said: It is desire, it is anger, born of the quality of passion (rajas), all-devouring, all-sinful. Know this to be the enemy here.

Explanation

Context

In Chapter 3, “Karma Yoga,” Arjuna is grappling with the dilemma of action versus renunciation. He initially questions why Krishna urges him to fight if the path of knowledge (renunciation) is superior. Krishna clarifies that true renunciation is not inaction, but rather performing one’s duties (karma) without attachment to the results. He emphasizes the importance of selfless action for cosmic order and individual liberation. After Krishna explains the nature of duty and the necessity of working for the welfare of the world, Arjuna poses a crucial question in verse 36: “But by what is a man impelled to commit sin, even unwillingly, as if by force?” This verse (3.37) is Krishna’s direct and profound answer, identifying the root cause of all unrighteous actions and the suffering that follows.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • काम (kāma): Desire, lust, craving, longing. This is not merely sensual desire, but any intense wanting or attachment to an outcome or object.
  • एष (eṣa): This, this one.
  • क्रोध (krodha): Anger, wrath, fury. This emotion typically arises when a desire is thwarted or unfulfilled.
  • रजोगुणसमुद्भवः (rajoguṇasamudbhavaḥ): Born of the quality of rajas. Rajas is one of the three gunas (qualities of material nature) — sattva (goodness), rajas (passion), and tamas (ignorance). Rajas is characterized by activity, ambition, attachment, and a drive for results. Desire and anger are direct manifestations of this passionate quality.
  • महाशनो (mahāśano): All-devouring, great eater, insatiable. It implies a hunger that can never be fully satisfied, consuming everything in its path.
  • महापाप्मा (mahāpāpmā): All-sinful, great sinner, highly destructive. It signifies the profound capacity of desire and anger to lead to actions that are morally wrong, harmful, and create negative karmic reactions.
  • विद्ध्येनमिह (viddhyenamiha): Know this here. Viddhi means know, understand clearly. Enam means this one, and iha means here, in this world or in this context of human experience.
  • वैरिणम् (vairiṇam): Enemy, foe. This highlights that desire and anger are not merely external challenges but internal adversaries that hinder our spiritual progress and peace.

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s answer is a cornerstone of the Gita’s practical philosophy. He directly attributes sinful actions, even those committed unwillingly, to kama (desire) and krodha (anger). These two are presented not as separate entities but as intimately linked, both arising from rajas.

Rajas is the force that propels us into activity, ambition, and the pursuit of objects and experiences. When this drive is directed externally and becomes attached to specific outcomes, it manifests as kama. The problem with kama is its insatiable nature, described as mahāśano – all-devouring. Fulfilling one desire only gives rise to another, often more intense, creating a never-ending cycle of wanting. This perpetual striving leads to anxiety, disappointment, and a sense of incompleteness.

When this kama is obstructed or not met, it transforms into krodha (anger). Anger is the painful reaction to thwarted desire, and it is equally destructive. Both desire and anger are labeled mahāpāpmā (all-sinful) because they are the root cause of actions that harm oneself and others, leading to negative karmic consequences and hindering spiritual growth. They cloud judgment, distort perception, and compel individuals to act against their own higher wisdom.

The most critical insight Krishna offers is that these forces are our vairiṇam – our greatest enemies, dwelling within us. Unlike external foes, which can be identified and confronted, these internal adversaries are far more insidious. They often masquerade as legitimate needs or impulses, making them difficult to recognize and overcome. Krishna’s teaching here is a profound call to self-awareness and introspection. Recognizing desire and anger as internal enemies is the crucial first step towards gaining mastery over them and achieving inner peace and liberation. This understanding is vital for karma yoga, as actions driven by kama and krodha lead to bondage, while actions performed with detachment, free from these internal enemies, lead to liberation.

Practical Application

  1. Mindful Observation of Impulses: When you feel a strong urge to act (e.g., buy something unnecessary, overeat, speak harshly), pause and identify the underlying emotion. Is it a desire for gratification (kama) or a surge of anger/frustration (krodha)? Simply recognizing these forces at play, rather than blindly acting on them, creates a space for conscious choice.
  2. Deconstructing Anger: When anger arises, instead of reacting immediately, try to trace it back to the thwarted desire. What expectation was not met? What control was lost? Understanding the root desire that led to anger can help you address the core issue – perhaps by adjusting expectations, practicing acceptance, or communicating needs constructively – rather than allowing anger to escalate.
  3. Cultivating Contentment: Practice appreciating what you have rather than constantly seeking what you lack. Engage in activities that bring genuine joy and fulfillment, rather than those driven by external validation or fleeting desires. This gradual shift in focus can diminish the power of insatiable kama and foster inner peace.

Reflection

  • What specific desires in my life often lead to frustration or anger?
  • How can I cultivate a greater sense of detachment from outcomes, thereby reducing the power of kama and krodha?
  • In what ways do I unknowingly allow these internal enemies to dictate my actions?

Key Concepts

  • Kama (Desire): The fundamental internal drive, characterized by insatiable craving, which is identified as a primary cause of suffering and unrighteous action.
  • Krodha (Anger): The destructive emotion that manifests when desires are obstructed, serving as a powerful internal enemy alongside kama.
  • Rajas (Passion): The quality of material nature from which both desire and anger originate, propelling activity but also binding the soul through attachment.
  • Internal Enemy: The crucial realization that our greatest adversaries are not external forces but the unchecked desires and anger residing within our own minds, requiring self-awareness and self-mastery to overcome.
