The Path to Immortality: Steadiness in Duality

Krishna explains that one who remains undisturbed by life's dualities of pain and pleasure attains the supreme state of immortality.

March 20, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga #Equanimity #Immortality #Duality
2.15

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 15

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

यं हि न व्यथयन्त्येते पुरुषं पुरुषर्षभ । समदुःखसुखं धीरं सोऽमृतत्वाय कल्पते ॥ १५ ॥

Transliteration (IAST)

yaṃ hi na vyathayantyete puruṣaṃ puruṣarṣabha | sama-duḥkha-sukhaṃ dhīraṃ so'mṛtatvāya kalpate || 15 ||

Translation

O best among men (Arjuna), indeed, the person whom these [sensory contacts] do not disturb, who is steady in pain and pleasure, that wise one is fit for immortality.

Explanation

Context

Verse 15 of the Bhagavad Gita’s second chapter, ‘Sankhya Yoga,’ continues Krishna’s profound discourse to Arjuna, who is overwhelmed by grief and delusion on the battlefield. In the preceding verses (11-13), Krishna introduced the fundamental distinction between the eternal Self (ātman) and the perishable body, emphasizing that the Self is never born nor dies. Verse 14 then explained that sensory contacts, which give rise to feelings of heat and cold, pleasure and pain, are transient and impermanent. He advised Arjuna to tolerate these fleeting experiences. Building directly upon this, Verse 15 elevates the discussion by revealing the ultimate reward for cultivating such tolerance and equanimity: the attainment of immortality. Krishna is systematically dismantling Arjuna’s attachment to the transient world and his fear of death, guiding him towards a deeper understanding of reality.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • यम् (yam): whom
  • हि (hi): indeed, certainly
  • न (na): not
  • व्यथयन्ति (vyathayanti): disturb, agitate
  • एते (ete): these (referring to the sensory contacts, pleasure, and pain mentioned in verse 14)
  • पुरुषम् (puruṣam): the person, the individual
  • पुरुषर्षभ (puruṣarṣabha): O best among men (an epithet for Arjuna)
  • समदुःखसुखम् (sama-duḥkha-sukham): one to whom pain and pleasure are the same; equanimous in pain and pleasure
  • धीरम् (dhīram): the steady, wise, resolute, or courageous one
  • सः (saḥ): he
  • अमृतत्वाय (amṛtatvāya): for immortality, for the state of being immortal
  • कल्पते (kalpate): is fit, is qualified, becomes capable

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s teaching in this verse is a cornerstone of Yogic philosophy. He asserts that true freedom and immortality are not found by escaping pain or endlessly pursuing pleasure, but by transcending their hold over our inner state. The phrase sama-duḥkha-sukham (equanimous in pain and pleasure) is crucial. It does not imply indifference or a lack of feeling, but rather a profound understanding that these experiences are external to the true Self and, like all phenomena of the material world, are impermanent. A dhīra – a wise and steady person – is one who has cultivated this discernment, recognizing the eternal nature of the Self amidst the fluctuating tides of worldly experience.

This equanimity is not a passive resignation but an active, conscious choice to remain centered. When one is not disturbed by the inevitable dualities of life (heat/cold, gain/loss, honor/dishonor, pleasure/pain), they are said to have realized the immortal nature of their own being. This amṛtatva (immortality) is not merely the absence of physical death; it is the realization of the Self as eternal, birthless, and deathless, thus achieving liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth and suffering (samsara).

This verse beautifully connects to other core themes of the Gita. It reinforces the Sankhya Yoga principle of discriminating between the eternal Spirit (Purusha) and transient Matter (Prakriti). It lays the philosophical groundwork for Karma Yoga, as an equanimous mind is essential for performing actions without attachment to their results. Furthermore, the wisdom (dhīra) described here is a hallmark of Jnana Yoga, the path of knowledge, where understanding the true nature of reality leads to liberation.

Practical Application

  1. Mindful Response to Challenges: When faced with a difficult situation – a professional setback, a personal loss, or physical discomfort – instead of being overwhelmed, pause and observe your internal reaction. Recognize that the feeling of distress is a temporary mental state, not an attack on your fundamental being. Practice acknowledging the pain without letting it completely define or destabilize you. This cultivates resilience and inner strength.
  2. Detachment from Outcomes: In moments of success or pleasure, observe the tendency to cling or identify too strongly with the external achievement. Similarly, during failures, notice the urge to self-criticize or despair. This verse encourages us to enjoy successes and learn from failures without letting either dictate our inner peace. For example, celebrate an achievement at work, but don’t let it inflate your ego or make you dependent on external validation. When a project fails, learn from it without internalizing the failure as a personal indictment.
  3. Cultivating Inner Stability: Practice techniques like meditation or deep breathing to anchor your consciousness. Regularly dedicating time to quiet contemplation can help develop the ‘dhīra’ quality – a steady intellect that observes life’s ups and downs from a place of inner calm. This practice helps to reduce reactivity and foster a more balanced perspective in daily interactions and decision-making.

Reflection

  • How often do I allow external circumstances (both positive and negative) to dictate my inner state of mind?
  • What specific situations in my life challenge my equanimity, and how can I practice a more balanced response?
  • What does ‘immortality’ mean to me beyond its literal interpretation, and how might cultivating equanimity lead to a sense of timelessness or liberation?

Key Concepts

  1. Equanimity (Samatva): The state of mental balance and steadiness amidst the dualities of pleasure and pain, gain and loss.
  2. Transcendence of Duality (Dvanda-atīta): The ability to move beyond being disturbed or swayed by the pairs of opposites inherent in worldly experience.
  3. Immortality (Amṛtatva): Not merely physical non-death, but the realization of the eternal, unchanging nature of the Self, leading to liberation from the cycle of birth and death.
  4. Wisdom/Steadiness (Dhīra): The discerning intellect and inner resolve that enables one to maintain equanimity and understand the true nature of reality.
