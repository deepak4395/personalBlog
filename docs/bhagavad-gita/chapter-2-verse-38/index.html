Embracing Equanimity: The Path to Action Without Sin (Bhagavad Gita 2.38)

Krishna instructs Arjuna to perform his duty with equanimity, treating pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat alike, thereby transcending sin.

April 11, 2026
#Bhagavad Gita #Sankhya Yoga
2.38

Chapter 2: Sankhya Yoga

Verse 38

Sanskrit (Devanagari)

सुखदुःखे समे कृत्वा लाभालाभौ जयाजयौ। ततो युद्धाय युज्यस्व नैवं पापमवाप्स्यसि॥

Transliteration (IAST)

sukha-duḥkhe same kṛtvā lābhālābhau jayājayau tato yuddhāya yujyasva naivaṁ pāpam avāpsyasi

Translation

Treating alike pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat, then engage in battle; by doing so, you will not incur sin.

Explanation

Context

This pivotal verse, Bhagavad Gita Chapter 2, Verse 38, marks a significant shift in Krishna’s counsel to Arjuna. Having previously expounded on the immortal nature of the soul (Sankhya philosophy) and the impermanence of the physical body, Krishna now begins to introduce the practical application of this wisdom in the realm of action, laying the groundwork for Karma Yoga. Arjuna is still gripped by moral dilemma and emotional turmoil, fearing the sin (pāpa) of killing his kinsmen and the resultant sorrow. Krishna, understanding Arjuna’s deep-seated anxieties about the consequences of war, offers a profound solution that transcends the duality of worldly experiences and liberates one from the karmic reactions of action. He is essentially telling Arjuna: “Since you are a warrior and your duty is to fight for righteousness, do so without attachment to the outcomes, and you will be free from sin.” This verse serves as a direct antidote to Arjuna’s fear of negative karma and a foundational principle for right action.

Word-by-Word Meaning

  • sukha-duḥkhe: ‘pleasure and pain.’ Sukha refers to happiness, joy, comfort; duḥkha refers to sorrow, suffering, discomfort. These are the twin experiences of human existence.
  • same kṛtvā: ‘treating alike,’ ‘making equal,’ ‘regarding with equanimity.’ Same means equal or alike; kṛtvā is a gerund meaning ‘having made’ or ‘treating.’ This implies a mental state of balance.
  • lābhālābhau: ‘gain and loss.’ A compound of lābha (gain, profit, acquisition) and alābha (loss, non-acquisition). These represent success and failure in material endeavors.
  • jayājayau: ‘victory and defeat.’ A compound of jaya (victory, triumph) and ajaya (defeat, non-victory). These pertain to the outcomes of conflict or competition.
  • tato: ‘then,’ ‘therefore.’ This indicates a consequence or a subsequent action based on the preceding instruction.
  • yuddhāya yujyasva: ‘engage in battle.’ Yuddhāya means ‘for battle’ or ‘to battle’; yujyasva is an imperative meaning ‘engage,’ ‘prepare yourself,’ ‘yoke yourself.’ It implies engaging with full commitment to the duty.
  • na evaṁ: ‘not thus,’ ‘in this manner, not.’ Na is ‘not’; evaṁ is ‘thus’ or ‘in this way.’
  • pāpam avāpsyasi: ‘you will incur sin.’ Pāpam refers to sin, demerit, negative karmic reaction; avāpsyasi is the future tense meaning ‘you will obtain’ or ‘you will incur.‘

Deeper Understanding

Krishna’s instruction here is a cornerstone of Karma Yoga – the path of action in wisdom. He is not advocating for indifference to action itself, but rather a profound detachment from the results of action. The core teaching is samatvam, or equanimity. This means cultivating a state of mind where one is undisturbed by the dualities of life – pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat. These are inherent in the material world, and trying to cling to one while avoiding the other is a futile exercise that leads to suffering and bondage.

Krishna is teaching Arjuna that true freedom from sin (pāpa) does not come from inaction or avoiding one’s duty, but from performing it with the right attitude. Sin, in this context, arises from attachment, selfish desire, and the ego’s identification with outcomes. When one acts with equanimity, dedicating the action to a higher purpose (dharma) without personal expectation for the fruits, the action itself becomes purifying. This is the essence of niṣkāma karma – action without desire for personal results. Arjuna’s dharma as a Kshatriya (warrior) is to fight for justice and uphold righteousness. By performing this duty with detachment from the outcomes, he transcends the karmic reactions that would otherwise bind him.

This verse beautifully connects to other Gita themes. It foreshadows the definition of Yoga as samatvam (equanimity) in verse 2.48. It reinforces the concept of dharma as one’s righteous duty, emphasizing that the manner of performance (with detachment) is as crucial as the action itself. By fostering inner balance, one rises above the cycle of elation and despair, achieving a stable, peaceful mind even amidst the greatest challenges.

Practical Application

  1. Professional Life: In your career, focus on performing your tasks with diligence, integrity, and your best effort, regardless of whether a project is lauded as a great success or deemed a failure. Accept promotions and setbacks with a balanced mind, learning from both without letting either define your self-worth or disrupt your inner peace. Your effort is within your control, the outcome often isn’t.
  2. Personal Relationships: When offering help or support to a friend or family member, do so with genuine compassion and without expectation of a specific outcome or reciprocation. If your efforts are met with gratitude, accept it. If they are met with indifference or even criticism, accept that too, maintaining your inner calm and the purity of your intention. The act of giving is the reward.
  3. Facing Daily Challenges: Life inevitably brings unexpected ups and downs – a sudden illness, a financial windfall, a broken appliance, a compliment, or an insult. Practice observing these events without immediately attaching strong emotional reactions. Can you acknowledge the experience without letting it hijack your mood for the entire day? This cultivation of equanimity allows you to navigate life’s fluctuations with greater resilience and inner stability.

Reflection

  • How often do I find my peace of mind disturbed by external events or the outcomes of my actions?
  • Can I identify areas in my life where I am overly attached to success or overly fearful of failure?
  • What would it mean for me to perform my duties with the same dedication, regardless of the anticipated pleasure or pain, gain or loss?

Key Concepts

  1. Equanimity (Samatvam): The state of mental balance and composure, treating dualities like pleasure/pain, gain/loss, victory/defeat with an even mind.
  2. Detachment from Results (Niṣkāma Karma): Performing one’s duty without personal desire or expectation for the fruits of action, thereby transcending karmic reactions.
  3. Righteous Action (Dharma): Engaging in one’s prescribed duty with full commitment, understanding that the manner of performance is crucial for spiritual liberation.
  4. Freedom from Sin (Pāpa): The understanding that sin arises from attachment and selfish motives, and can be avoided by acting with equanimity and detachment.
